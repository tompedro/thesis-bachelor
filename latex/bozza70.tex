The previous chapter showed an algebraic reformulation of quantum mechanics that only addressed the time-independent axiomatics. Now, we ask how a quantum system evolves and how its continuous symmetries manifest. Instead of postulating a separate equation of motion or invoking group representations on a Hilbert space, we derive both the dynamics and the symmetry principles directly from the geometry of the space of physical states.

The starting point is the set of pure states, which, after isolating a single superselection sector, becomes a projective Hilbert space. This manifold carries a natural geometric structure that combines symplectic, Riemannian, and complex data in a mutually compatible way, called, a Kähler structure. Each of these geometric ingredients plays a distinct physical role. The symplectic form provides the Poisson bracket that generates time evolution. Given the energy operator, its mean value becomes a smooth function on the state space, and the associated Hamiltonian vector field defines a flow whose integral curves are the solutions of the Schrödinger equation.

The Riemannian metric captures the statistical and probabilistic aspects of the theory. It induces a notion of distance between pure states, and the squared geodesic separation between two neighbouring states equals the transition probability predicted by the Born rule. Moreover, the process of projecting onto a subspace after a measurement can be reinterpreted geometrically as the operation of finding the point on the corresponding submanifold of eigenspaces that lies closest to the pre‑measurement state in the sense of this Riemannian distance. 

At the end, continuous symmetries of the system, such as rotations or translations, are implemented as flows generated by such admissible functions. Thanks to geometry, we can assert the existence of a momentum map assigning to each symmetry generator a conserved quantity. This construction yields a geometric version of Noether’s theorem, where the conservation laws emerge from the equivariance properties of the momentum map, again independently of the Hilbert space representation. The entire structure thus follows from the intrinsic geometry of the state manifold, providing a fully covariant formulation of quantum mechanics.
	
\section{Kähler Geometry}
\label{sec:almost-kahler-hilbertian}
The mathematical framework used in this chapter is generalization of the one used in Chapter \ref{chp:1}. We see that differential manifolds can be stated substituting open sets of $\R^n$ as the image of charts of the maximal atlas. In fact, to generalize differential geometry to complex numbers, we need an operation that mimics the complex unit, called a complex structure. Together with a particular metric and manifold, this will create the key structure of the chapter, a Kähler manifold. This mathematical concept is rich enough to accommodate the whole framework of quantum mechanics, as we will see in the next sections.

We start by modeling a smooth manifold onto an Hilbert space, as per Definition \ref{def:hilbert}. This statement follows Definition \ref{def:smooth-man}, still we need to ensure compatibility with the structures already imposed on the Hilbert space. 

\begin{definition}[Hilbertian manifold]
	\label{def:hilbert-manifold}
	A real smooth Hilbertian manifold $\mathcal{M}$ modeled on a real Hilbert space $\mathbb{H}$, as per Definition \ref{def:hilbert}, is a smooth manifold, as per Definition \ref{def:smooth-man}, whose maximal atlas $\mathcal{A} = \{(U_\alpha, \phi_\alpha)\}_{\alpha \in I}$, as in Definition \ref{def:maximal-atlas}, satisfies the following conditions.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item For every $\alpha \in I$, the image $\phi_\alpha(U_\alpha)$ is an open subset of $\mathbb{H}$.
		\item For every $\alpha, \beta \in I$ such that $U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \neq \emptyset$, the transition map $\phi_\beta \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1}$ is of class $C^\infty$ in the sense of the Fréchet derivative.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

For a real smooth Hilbertian manifold $\mathcal{M}$, the following notation for
bundles of multilinear maps is used throughout.

\begin{definition}[Families of multilinear maps]
	\label{def:families-multilinear}
	Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a real smooth Hilbertian manifold, as per Definition \ref{def:hilbert-manifold}. For each $x \in \mathcal{M}$, consider the Banach spaces of bounded linear operators on $T_x\mathcal{M}$, of bounded linear maps $T_x^*\mathcal{M}\to T_x\mathcal{M}$, and of bounded $n$-linear forms on $T_x\mathcal{M}$. The disjoint unions of these spaces over all $x \in \mathcal{M}$ are denoted, respectively, by
	\[
		\B(T\mathcal{M}) = \bigsqcup_{x \in \mathcal{M}} \B(T_x\mathcal{M}), \qquad \B(T^*\mathcal{M},T\mathcal{M}) = \bigsqcup_{x \in \mathcal{M}} \B(T_x^*\mathcal{M},T_x\mathcal{M}) , \qquad \B_n(T\mathcal{M},\R) =  \bigsqcup_{x \in \mathcal{M}}\B_n(T_x\mathcal{M},\R).
	\]
\end{definition}

\noindent With this notation available, the almost complex structures
needed to define an almost Kähler manifold can be introduced as smooth
sections of $\B(T\mathcal{M})$ squaring to $-1$.

\begin{definition}[Almost complex structure]
	\label{def:almost-complex-structure}
	Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a real smooth Hilbert manifold, as per Definition \ref{def:hilbert-manifold}. An almost complex structure on $\mathcal{M}$ is a smooth section $J: \mathcal{M} \to \B(T\mathcal{M})$, as per Definition \ref{def:smooth-section}, which is required that for every $x \in \mathcal{M}$, the image operator $J_x \in \B(T\mathcal{M})$ satisfies the property $$J_x \circ J_x = -\Id_{T_x\mathcal{M}}.$$
\end{definition}

Consequently, the map $J$ endows each real tangent space with the algebraic structure of a complex vector space playing the role of the imaginary unit.

\begin{example}
	On $\R^{2n}\cong\C^n$ with real coordinates $(x^1,\dots,x^n,y^1,\dots,y^n)$
	and $z^j=x^j+iy^j$, the linear map defined pointwise by
	$J\partial_{x^j}=\partial_{y^j}$, $J\partial_{y^j}=-\partial_{x^j}$
	satisfies $J^2=-\Id$, so it is an almost complex structure as per
	Definition~\ref{def:almost-complex-structure}. It is the
	endomorphism induced by multiplication by $i$ on $\C^n$ under the identification $T_z\C^n\cong\C^n$, and its integrability, in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:integrable-acs} comes from the trivial holomorphic atlas of $\C^n$.
\end{example}

Not every almost complex structure comes from an underlying complex
manifold structure. The obstruction is measured by a torsion tensor,
and the case in which it vanishes deserves its own name because it is
the case relevant to Kähler geometry.

\begin{definition}[Integrable almost complex structure]
	\label{def:integrable-acs}
	Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a real smooth Hilbert manifold, as per Definition \ref{def:hilbert-manifold}. An almost complex structure $J$ as per Definition~\ref{def:almost-complex-structure} is integrable if its torsion vanishes. That is, if for every open set $\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{M}$ and every pair of smooth vector fields $X,Y$ on $\mathcal{U}$,
	\[
		[JX,JY]-[X,Y]-J[X,JY]-J[JX,Y]=0.
	\]
\end{definition}

\noindent The compatibility condition between an almost complex structure and a Riemannian metric that defines an almost Kähler manifold can now be stated.

\begin{definition}[Riemannian metric]
	\label{def:riemannian-metric}
	Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a real smooth Hilbertian manifold, as per Definition \ref{def:hilbert-manifold}. A Riemannian metric on $\mathcal{M}$ is a smooth section $g: \mathcal{M} \to \B_2(T\mathcal{M}, \R)$, as in Definition \ref{def:families-multilinear}, that, for each $x \in \mathcal{M}$, the evaluation $g_x : T_x\mathcal{M} \times T_x\mathcal{M} \to \R$ is a symmetric, continuous, and positive-definite bilinear form. 
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Local coordinate expression and line element]
	\label{rem:metric-coordinates}
	In a local coordinate chart $(U, x)$ with real coordinates $(x^1, \dots, x^n)$, the Riemannian metric $g$ is a symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor field, as in Definition \ref{def:tensor-field}. It is expressed via the tensor product as
	\begin{equation}
		g = \sum_{i,j=1}^n g_{ij} dx^i \otimes dx^j,
	\end{equation}
	where $g_{ij} := g\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}, \frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\right)$ are the smooth metric components and $\{dx^i\}$ constitutes the dual basis of the cotangent space. In physics literature, the metric is conventionally denoted by the infinitesimal line element $ds^2$:
	\begin{equation}
		ds^2 = \sum_{i,j=1}^n g_{ij} dx^i dx^j.
	\end{equation}
	In this notation, the juxtaposition $dx^i dx^j$ is a formal abbreviation for the symmetric tensor product $\frac{1}{2}(dx^i \otimes dx^j + dx^j \otimes dx^i)$. Consequently, expressions such as $dx^2$ strictly denote the rank-two tensor $dx \otimes dx$. When evaluated on a tangent vector field, this tensor returns the square of the vector's spatial projection along the associated coordinate axis.
\end{remark}

Having set all the structures needed, we are ready to enrich an Hilbertian manifold with an almost complex structure and a Riemannian metric. This generates an almost Kähler manifold.

\begin{definition}[Almost Kähler manifold]
	\label{def:almost-kahler-manifold}
	An almost Kähler manifold is a triple $(\mathcal{M},J,g)$, where $\mathcal{M}$ is a real
	smooth Hilbertian manifold,as per Definition \ref{def:hilbert-manifold}, $J$ is an almost complex structure as per Definition~\ref{def:almost-complex-structure}, and $g$ is a Riemann metric, as in Definition \ref{def:riemannian-metric} such that
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item $g$ is $J$-invariant, that is $g_x(J_xX_x,J_xY_x)=g_x(X_x,Y_x)$ for all $x\in\mathcal{M}$, $X_x,Y_x\in T_x\mathcal{M}$.
		\item The fundamental form defined via $\omega_x(X_x,Y_x):=g_x(J_xX_x,Y_x)$ is closed.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:almost-kahler-meaning}
	Definition \ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold} intertwines Riemannian, complex, and symplectic structures. 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Condition (i) makes $J$ a metric isometry, yielding an almost Hermitian structure. 
		\item Condition (ii) ensures the fundamental $2$-form $\omega=g(J\cdot,\cdot)$ is closed and hence non-degenerate, elevating it to a symplectic form. Thus an almost Kähler manifold is a symplectic manifold, as in Definition \ref{def:sympl-man}, with compatible $J$ and metric $g$.
	\end{itemize} 
	Physically, the triad $(g,J,\omega)$ unifies Hamiltonian mechanics, via $\omega$, quantum state distances/transition probabilities via $g$, and complex phase via $J$. The qualifier “almost” reflects that $J$ need not be integrable, \textit{i.e.}, its torsion tensor need not vanish.
\end{remark}

Once the almost complex structure of an almost Kähler manifold is required to be integrable in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:integrable-acs}, the manifold acquires an underlying holomorphic structure and deserves the sharper name of Kähler manifold, which is the setting in which the projective Hilbert space of a quantum system will be placed.

\begin{definition}[Kähler manifold]
	\label{def:kahler-manifold-hilbertian}
	An almost Kähler manifold $(\mathcal{M},J,g)$ as per
	Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold} is called a Kähler
	manifold if its almost complex structure $J$ is integrable, as per
	Definition~\ref{def:integrable-acs}.
\end{definition}

\noindent The fundamental form $\omega$ and the metric $g$ of an
almost Kähler manifold each induce an isomorphism from the cotangent
to the tangent space at every point, and it is these two musical
isomorphisms, rather than $\omega$ and $g$ themselves, that will enter
directly into the definition of the Poisson and Riemann brackets.

\begin{definition}[Musical isomorphisms of an almost Kähler manifold]
	\label{def:musical-isomorphisms}
	Let $(\mathcal{M},J,g)$ be an almost Kähler manifold as per
	Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold}, with fundamental form
	$\omega$. The musical isomorphisms $I_x,G_x:T_x^*\mathcal{M}\to T_x\mathcal{M}$, for
	$x\in\mathcal{M}$, are the topological-linear isomorphisms defined implicitly
	by
	\begin{equation}
		\omega_x(I_x\alpha_x,X_x)=\langle\alpha_x,X_x\rangle,\qquad g_x(G_x\alpha_x,X_x)=\langle\alpha_x,X_x\rangle,
	\end{equation}
	for $\alpha_x\in T_x^*\mathcal{M}$, $X_x\in T_x\mathcal{M}$. The corresponding smooth
	sections of $\B(T^*\mathcal{M},T\mathcal{M})$ are denoted $I,G$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	\label{rem:G-JI}
	The two musical isomorphisms of Definition~\ref{def:musical-isomorphisms}
	are related by $G=J\circ I$. Indeed, for $\alpha_x\in T_x^*\mathcal{M}$ and
	$X_x\in T_x\mathcal{M}$, the definitions of $\omega$ and $I_x$ yield
	\begin{equation}
		g_x(J_xI_x\alpha_x,X_x)=\omega_x(I_x\alpha_x,X_x)=\langle\alpha_x,X_x\rangle=g_x(G_x\alpha_x,X_x),
	\end{equation}
	and non-degeneracy of $g_x$ entails that $J_xI_x\alpha_x=G_x\alpha_x$
	for every $\alpha_x$, that is, $G=J\circ I$.
\end{remark}

With the musical isomorphisms in place, two bilinear brackets between
smooth functions can be defined, one recovering the ordinary
symplectic Poisson bracket and the other, depending only on the
metric, providing the dispersion structure that will later encode the
probabilistic content of quantum mechanics.

\begin{definition}[Poisson and Riemann brackets]
	\label{def:poisson-riemann-bracket}
	Let $(\mathcal{M},J,g)$ be an almost Kähler manifold as per
	Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold}, and let $f,l\in
	C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\R)$. The Poisson bracket $\poisson{f}{l} \in C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\R)$ and the Riemann
	bracket $((f,l)) \in C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\R)$ are defined via
	\begin{equation}
		\poisson{f}{l}:=\langle df,I\,dl\rangle,\qquad ((f,l)):=\langle df,G\,dl\rangle.
	\end{equation}
	In which, $I,G$ are the musical isomorphisms of
	Definition~\ref{def:musical-isomorphisms}, and $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ is the inner product between forms and vectors. By bilinearity, both
	extend to smooth complex-valued functions on $\mathcal{M}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
	On $\R^2$, identified with $\C$ via $z=x+iy$, define a 2-form, as in Definition \ref{def:forms} via $\omega=dx\wedge dy$ and define a Riemannian metric, as in Definition \ref{def:riemannian-metric} as $g=dx^2+dy^2$. In addition, let $J$ be a complex structure, as in Definition \ref{def:almost-complex-structure}  via $J\partial_x=\partial_y$. 
	
	For $f=x$, $l=y$,
	one has $df=dx$, $dl=dy$, and $I\,dl=-\partial_x$, $G\,dl=\partial_y$
	from Definition~\ref{def:musical-isomorphisms}. Hence
	$\poisson{f}{l}=\langle dx,-\partial_x\rangle=-1$ recovers, up to sign
	convention, the canonical Poisson bracket, while
	$((f,l))=\langle dx,\partial_y\rangle=0$, since $df$ and $dl$ are
	$g$-orthogonal. The two coordinate functions are canonically
	conjugate but statistically independent.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
	\label{rem:poisson-riemann-reformulation}
	The Poisson bracket of Definition~\ref{def:poisson-riemann-bracket} is
	the standard symplectic bracket associated with $\omega$ as in Definition \ref{def:poisson-bracket}, while the
	Riemann bracket, depending only on $g$, is not a symplectic
	invariant. Using $G=J\circ I$, as per Remark~\ref{rem:G-JI}, together
	with the $J$-invariance of $\omega$ and $g$,
	\begin{equation}
		\poisson{f}{l}=\omega(Idf,Idl)=\omega(Gdf,Gdl),\qquad ((f,l))=g(Gdf,Gdl)=g(Idf,Idl).
	\end{equation}
\end{remark}

\noindent The Poisson and Riemann brackets combine into a single
complex-valued bracket, and together with the ordinary pointwise
product they generate two further bilinear products on smooth
functions, the $\circ_\nu$ and $*_\nu$ products, that will be the
geometric counterpart of the anticommutator and of the operator
product once the manifold is specialized to $\PH$.

\begin{definition}[Kähler bracket, $\circ_\nu$ and $*_\nu$ products]
	\label{def:kahler-bracket-products}
	Let $(\mathcal{M},J,g)$ be an almost Kähler manifold as per
	Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold}, and let $f,l\in
	C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\C)$. The Kähler bracket between $f$ and $l$ is
	\begin{equation}
		\langle f,l\rangle:=((f,l))+i\poisson{f}{l},
	\end{equation}
	where $\poisson{\cdot}{\cdot}$ and $((\cdot,\cdot))$ are the brackets
	of Definition~\ref{def:poisson-riemann-bracket}. For $\nu\in\R$, the
	$\circ_\nu$ product and the $*_\nu$ product between $f$ and $l$ are
	the smooth complex functions
	\begin{equation}
		f\circ_\nu l:=\tfrac12\nu((f,l))+fl,\qquad f*_\nu l:=\tfrac12\nu\langle f,l\rangle+fl.
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	\label{rem:algebra-structures}
	Equipped with the Riemann bracket, or alternatively with the
	$\circ_\nu$ product, $C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\R)$, respectively $C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\C)$, is a real, respectively complex, commutative, nonassociative algebra. The Kähler bracket and the $*_\nu$ product provide two further complex,
	nonassociative algebra structures on $C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\C)$. With either of
	these two products and with complex conjugation as involution, $C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\C)$ is an involutive algebra \cite[p.~2892]{CirelliManiaPizzocchero1990}.
\end{remark}

\noindent The four products introduced in
Definition~\ref{def:kahler-bracket-products} are not independent. Each
can be recovered from any other two, a fact that will be used repeatedly to move between the symplectic and the Jordan-like descriptions of the same algebraic structure.

\begin{proposition}
	\label{prop:kahler-products-identities}
	Let $(\mathcal{M},J,g)$ be an almost Kähler manifold as per
	Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold}, and let $f,l\in
	C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\C)$. Then, with $\circ_\nu$, $*_\nu$ and
	$\poisson{\cdot}{\cdot}$ as per Definitions~\ref{def:poisson-riemann-bracket}
	and~\ref{def:kahler-bracket-products},
	\begin{align}
		f*_\nu l&=f\circ_\nu l+\tfrac{i}{2}\nu\poisson{f}{l},\\
		f\circ_\nu l&=\tfrac12(f*_\nu l+l*_\nu f),\\
		\poisson{f}{l}&=\tfrac{1}{i\nu}(f*_\nu l-l*_\nu f)
	\end{align}
	\cite[Eqs.~(2.10)--(2.12)]{CirelliManiaPizzocchero1990}.
\end{proposition}

\noindent Among the smooth functions on an almost Kähler manifold, a
distinguished class is singled out by the requirement that its
Hamiltonian flow preserve the metric, and not only the symplectic
form. This is the class that will be identified with the observables of a quantum system.

\begin{definition}[Kählerian function]
	\label{def:kahlerian-function}
	Let $(\mathcal{M},J,g)$ be an almost Kähler manifold as per
	Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold}, let $f\in
	C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\R)$, and let $\Ham{f}:=Idf$, with $I$ as per
	Definition~\ref{def:musical-isomorphisms}. The function $f$ is
	Kählerian if $\mathcal{L}_{\Ham{f}}g=0$, where $\mathcal{L}$ denotes the Lie derivative, as in Definition \ref{def:lie-dericative}.
	If $f\in C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\C)$, $f$ is Kählerian if $\Re f$ and $\Im f$ are.
	The set of Kählerian functions is denoted $\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{M},\R)$, respectively
	$\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{M},\C)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	\label{rem:kahlerian-hamiltonian-flow}
	The vector field $\Ham{f}=Idf$ of Definition~\ref{def:kahlerian-function}
	is the Hamiltonian vector field of $f$, and the condition
	$\mathcal{L}_{\Ham{f}}g=0$ states that the Hamiltonian flow of $f$ preserves the
	metric $g$. Since $\omega$ is closed and $\iota_{\Ham{f}}\omega=df$ by
	Definition~\ref{def:hamiltonian-vec-field}, Cartan's formula Equation \eqref{eq:cartan} yields $$\mathcal{L}_{\Ham{f}}\omega=d\iota_{\Ham{f}}\omega+\iota_{\Ham{f}}d\omega=d(df)=0.$$ The flow automatically preserves the fundamental form. Moreover, from $\mathcal{L}_{\Ham{f}}g=0$ it then follows that $\mathcal{L}_{\Ham{f}}J=0$ as well, because $J$ is determined by $\omega$ and $g$ through
	Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold}. Therefore, if $f$ is
	Kählerian, its Hamiltonian flow preserves the whole Kähler structure.
\end{remark}

\noindent The Kählerian functions of Definition~\ref{def:kahlerian-function} are closed under the Poisson bracket.

\begin{proposition}
	\label{prop:kahlerian-lie-subalgebra}
	$\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{M},\R)$ as in Definition \ref{def:kahlerian-function}, respectively $\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{M},\C)$, is a Lie subalgebra of $C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\R)$, respectively $C^\infty(\mathcal{M},\C)$, with respect to the Poisson bracket of Definition~\ref{def:poisson-riemann-bracket}. 
\end{proposition}

\subsection*{Complex manifolds and the Dolbeault operators}
\label{sec:complex-manifolds-dolbeault}

The definitions and theorems of the preceding sections can appear abstract when viewed through the lens of pure linear algebra on the Hilbert space $\H$. To ground these concepts and show the way for explicit computations, it is essential to examine the local coordinate picture. The fundamental simplification offered by Kähler geometry is the reduction of a complex, multi-component geometric object to a single, real-valued scalar function, called, the Kähler potential.

We start by setting the definition of a complex manifold which is the extension of a real manifold to complex numbers.

\begin{definition}[Complex manifold]
	\label{def:complex-manifold}
	A complex manifold of complex dimension $n$ is a smooth manifold $\mathcal{M}$, as in Definition \ref{def:smooth-man}, of real dimension $2n$ together with an atlas of charts $\varphi_\alpha:U_\alpha\to\C^n$, $\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha$ such that every transition map $\varphi_\beta\circ\varphi_\alpha^{-1}$, between open subsets of $\C^n$, is holomorphic. Such an atlas is called a holomorphic atlas for $\mathcal{M}$.
\end{definition}

\noindent A holomorphic atlas as per Definition~\ref{def:complex-manifold}
determines an integrable almost complex structure on $\mathcal{M}$, obtained by
transporting multiplication by $i$ on $\C^n$ through each chart. The
converse, that every integrable almost complex structure arises this
way, is the content of the Newlander-Nirenberg theorem, recalled here
without proof.

\begin{theorem}[Newlander-Nirenberg]
	\label{thm:newlander-nirenberg}
	Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a smooth manifold of real dimension $2n$ equipped with an
	integrable almost complex structure $J$, as per
	Definition~\ref{def:integrable-acs}. Then $\mathcal{M}$ admits a holomorphic
	atlas, as per Definition~\ref{def:complex-manifold}, inducing $J$
	\cite[Theorem~1.4]{Moroianu2004}.
\end{theorem}

\noindent Fix a complex manifold $\mathcal{M}$ of complex dimension $n$ as per
Definition~\ref{def:complex-manifold}, and a local holomorphic chart
$(U,\varphi)$ with complex coordinates $z=(z^1,\dots,z^n)\in\C^n$, so
that $z^j=x^j+iy^j$, where $(x^1,\dots,x^n,y^1,\dots,y^n)$ are the
corresponding local real coordinates on $U$. The complexified
cotangent space at each point of $U$ then splits according to the
holomorphic and antiholomorphic differentials, and this splitting is
what allows differential forms to be assigned a bidegree.

\begin{definition}[Forms of type $(p,q)$]
	\label{def:pq-forms}
	Let $(U,\varphi)$ be a local holomorphic chart on a complex manifold
	$\mathcal{M}$, as per Definition~\ref{def:complex-manifold}, with complex
	coordinates $z=(z^1,\dots,z^n)$. The subspaces $\Lambda^{1,0}$ and
	$\Lambda^{0,1}$ of the complexified cotangent space $T^*\mathcal{M}\otimes\C$
	at each point of $U$ are the spans of $\{dz^1,\dots,dz^n\}$ and of
	$\{d\bar z^1,\dots,d\bar z^n\}$, respectively. As differential
	form on $U$ is of type $(p,q)$ if, at every point, it lies in
	\begin{equation}
		\Lambda^{p,q}:=\underbrace{\Lambda^{1,0}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Lambda^{1,0}}_{p}\wedge\underbrace{\Lambda^{0,1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Lambda^{0,1}}_{q}.
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\noindent The decomposition of forms by type, as per
Definition~\ref{def:pq-forms}, is compatible with the exterior
derivative, and this compatibility is what makes it possible to split
$d$ into a holomorphic and an antiholomorphic piece.

\begin{definition}[Dolbeault operators]
	\label{def:dolbeault-operators}
	Let $(U,\varphi)$ be a local holomorphic chart on a complex manifold
	$\mathcal{M}$, as per Definition~\ref{def:complex-manifold}, with complex
	coordinates $z=(z^1,\dots,z^n)$. On $C^\infty(U,\C)$, the Dolbeault
	operators $\partial,\bar\partial$ are the linear differential
	operators defined locally by
	\begin{equation}
		\partial f := \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial z^j} dz^j, \qquad \bar{\partial} f := \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial \bar{z}^j} d\bar{z}^j,
	\end{equation}
	extended to forms of type $(p,q)$, as per Definition~\ref{def:pq-forms},
	by $d=\partial+\bar\partial$, with $\partial$ raising $p$ by one and
	$\bar\partial$ raising $q$ by one.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	\label{rem:dolbeault-nilpotency}
	The nilpotency of the exterior derivative, $d^2=0$, together with the
	splitting $d=\partial+\bar\partial$ of Definition~\ref{def:dolbeault-operators},
	yields $0=d^2=\partial^2+\bar\partial^2+(\partial\bar\partial+\bar\partial\partial)$.
	Since $\partial^2$, $\bar\partial^2$ and $\partial\bar\partial+\bar\partial\partial$
	raise the bidegree $(p,q)$ of a form to $(p+2,q)$, $(p,q+2)$ and
	$(p+1,q+1)$ respectively, these three subspaces intersect trivially,
	so each term vanishes separately:
	\begin{equation}
		\partial^2=0, \qquad \bar\partial^2=0, \qquad \partial\bar\partial+\bar\partial\partial=0.
	\end{equation}
\end{remark}

\noindent A closed real $(1,1)$-form on a complex manifold, such as
the fundamental form of a Kähler manifold, admits a local description in terms of the Dolbeault operators just introduced, which is the analytic fact underlying the notion of Kähler potential.

\begin{theorem}[Local $\partial\bar\partial$-lemma]
	\label{thm:ddbar-lemma}
	Let $\omega$ be a real, closed differential form of type $(1,1)$, as
	per Definition~\ref{def:pq-forms}, on a complex manifold $\mathcal{M}$, as per
	Definition~\ref{def:complex-manifold}. For every point $p \in \mathcal{M}$,
	there exists a contractible local holomorphic chart $(U, z)$
	containing $p$ and a smooth real-valued function $K \in
	C^\infty(U, \R)$ such that, on $U$,
	\begin{equation}
		\omega = i \partial \bar{\partial} K
	\end{equation}
	\cite[Proposition~2.8]{Moroianu2004}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Kähler potential]
	\label{def:kahler-potential}
	Let $\omega$ be a real, closed $(1,1)$-form on a complex manifold
	$\mathcal{M}$, as per Definition~\ref{def:complex-manifold}, and let $(U,z)$ be
	a local holomorphic chart. A smooth function $K\in C^\infty(U,\R)$
	such that $\omega=i\partial\bar\partial K$ on $U$, as provided by
	Theorem~\ref{thm:ddbar-lemma}, is called a local Kähler potential for
	$\omega$ on $(U,z)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	\label{rem:kahler-potential-uniqueness}
	The local Kähler potential of Definition~\ref{def:kahler-potential} is not unique. In fact, if $K$ and $K'$ are both local Kähler potentials for $\omega$ on $(U,z)$, then $\partial\bar\partial(K-K')=0$ on $U$, so that $K-K'$ coincides with the real part of a holomorphic function. Conversely, adding to $K$ the real part of any holomorphic function on $U$ produces another local Kähler potential for $\omega$.
\end{remark}

\noindent Once a local Kähler potential is available, the whole Riemannian metric tensor of a Kähler manifold as in Definition~\ref{def:kahler-manifold-hilbertian}, restricted to a finite-dimensional holomorphic chart, is determined by the mixed second-order derivatives of that single scalar function.

\begin{theorem}[Local coordinate expression of the metric]
	\label{thm:metric-local-expression}
	Let $(\mathcal{M}, J, g)$ be a Kähler manifold, as per Definition~\ref{def:kahler-manifold-hilbertian}, of finite complex dimension $n$, and let $K$ be a local Kähler potential, as per Definition~\ref{def:kahler-potential}, for the fundamental form $\omega$ on a chart $(U, z)$. The components of the metric tensor $g$ in the coordinates $(z^1, \dots, z^n)$ are given by
	\begin{equation}
		g_{j\bar{k}} = \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial z^j \partial \bar{z}^k},
	\end{equation}
	and the resulting Hermitian matrix $(g_{j\bar{k}})$ is positive definite everywhere on $U$
	\cite[\S~5.2]{Moroianu2004}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
	\label{rem:metric-positive-definite}
	The positive definiteness recorded in Theorem~\ref{thm:metric-local-expression}, in the coordinates $(z^1,\dots,z^n)$, is equivalent to the requirement $g(X,X)>0$ for every nonzero tangent vector $X$, which is already part of $g$ being a Riemannian metric on $\mathcal{M}$ as per Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold}.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Local Expression of Curvature}
\noindent The same local potential that determines the metric also determines its curvature. This geometrical intrinsic object measures the local deviation from a flat Euclidean space. Since the metric components $g_{j\bar{k}}$ are defined by the second derivatives of the Kähler potential $K$, the local obstruction to flattening the metric relies on the second derivatives of $g_{j\bar{k}}$. Consequently, the Riemann curvature tensor is algebraically encoded within the fourth-order mixed partial derivatives of the single scalar function $K$.

\begin{definition}[Riemann curvature via Kähler potential]
	\label{def:riemann-curvature}
	Let $(\mathcal{M}, J, g)$ be a Kähler manifold, as in Definition \ref{def:kahler-manifold-hilbertian} with local holomorphic
	coordinates $(z^1, \dots, z^n)$ and Kähler potential $K$, as in Definition \ref{def:kahler-potential}. The components of the Riemann curvature tensor evaluated on holomorphic planes are uniquely determined by the metric components $$g_{j\bar{k}} = \partial_j \partial_{\bar{k}} K.$$
\end{definition}

\noindent In a holomorphic chart centered at a point, the Kähler potential can always be chosen so that the metric components satisfy \(g_{j\bar{k}}(0)=\delta_{jk}\) and all first derivatives vanish at the origin. This is equivalent to the freedom to add the real part of a holomorphic function to \(K\). In such adapted coordinates, the curvature tensor depends on the fourth derivatives of \(K\), with no lower-order interference.

At any local origin $z=0$, where the first derivatives of the metric vanish, $\partial_m g_{j\bar{k}}|_0 = 0$, the curvature tensor components reduce to
\[
	R_{j\bar{k}m\bar{l}} = -\frac{\partial^2 g_{j\bar{k}}}{\partial z^m \partial \bar{z}^l} = -\frac{\partial^4 K}{\partial z^j \partial \bar{z}^k \partial z^m \partial \bar{z}^l}.
\]

\noindent Contracting \(R_{j\bar{k}m\bar{l}}\) with \(X\) and \(\bar{X}\), normalised by \(g(X,X)\), yields the holomorphic sectional curvature. That is the curvature along the complex curve tangent to \(X\). This scalar will characterise \(\PH\) in Theorem~\ref{thm:hawley-igusa} once shown constant.

\begin{definition}[Holomorphic sectional curvature]\label{def:holomorphic-curvature}
	Let $(\mathcal{M}, J, g)$ be a Kähler manifold, as in Definition \ref{def:kahler-manifold-hilbertian} with Riemann curvature as in Definition \ref{def:riemann-curvature}.  Given a tangent vector $X = \sum v^j \partial_{z^j}$, the \emph{holomorphic sectional curvature} $H(X)$ is the normalized restriction of the curvature tensor along the complex line generated by $X$:
	\begin{equation}
	H(X) := \frac{\sum R_{j\bar{k}m\bar{l}} v^j \bar{v}^k v^m \bar{v}^l}{\left( \sum g_{j\bar{k}} v^j \bar{v}^k \right)^2}.\label{eq:sec-curv}
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\noindent Before curving the manifold, it is worth checking that the formula returns the answer expected of the flat model, which carries no curvature at all.

\begin{example}[Flat space]
	\label{ex:flat-space-curvature}
	On $\C^n$ with the flat Kähler potential $K(z,\bar z)=\|z\|^2$, the
	metric components are $g_{j\bar{k}}=\partial_j\partial_{\bar{k}}K=\delta_{jk}$,
	constant on all of $\C^n$. Every derivative of $g_{j\bar{k}}$
	vanishes identically, so $R_{j\bar{k}m\bar{l}}=0$ and $H(X)=0$ for
	every tangent vector $X$, as expected of Euclidean space regarded as
	a Kähler manifold.
\end{example}

The local Kähler potential thus determines the entire geometry, metric and curvature alike. The non-vanishing terms at the fourth order of the Taylor expansion of $K$ provide the exact fundamental obstruction to mapping the manifold globally to a Euclidean space. This algebraic reduction establishes the explicit starting point for evaluating the constant holomorphic sectional curvature of the projective Hilbert space in Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-curvature-lambda} below.

\section{The Projective Hilbert Space as a Kähler Manifold}
\label{sec:ph-kahler}
We now specialise the general Kähler framework to the manifold of physical interest, namely the projective Hilbert space. This space is obtained by quotienting the nonzero vectors of a complex Hilbert space by complex scaling. We show that the quotient is the pure state space of the algebra of compact operators. Equipped with a holomorphic atlas, it acquires a complex structure, while the underlying inner product naturally induces both a Riemannian metric and a compatible symplectic form. These two structures, together with the complex structure already present, form the Fubini–Study geometry, which is the canonical Kähler structure of the pure state space. Then, we derive their explicit local coordinate expressions. This is achieved through the introduction of a global Kähler potential on the chart, as we seen in the previous section.

We start by stating the main protagonist of this section, the projective Hilbert space.

\begin{definition}[Projective Hilbert space]
	\label{def:projective-hilbert-space}
	Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space. On $\H^*:=\H\setminus\{0\}$ define
	$\Psi\sim\Phi$ if $\Phi=z\Psi$ for some $z\in\C\setminus\{0\}$. The
	\emph{projective Hilbert space} is
	\[
	\PH:=\H^*/\sim,
	\]
	with $[\Psi]$ the equivalence class of $\Psi\in\H^*$.
\end{definition}

Recalling the algebraic framework, we show that the pure states of a $C^*$-algebra can be rewritten as a union of projective Hilbert spaces. Where, each one of the Hilbert spaces is the result of an irreducible representation constructed via GNS Theorem \ref{thm:gns}.

\begin{theorem}[Pure state decomposition]\label{thm:pure-state-decomposition}
	The pure state space $\mathcal{P}(\A)$ of a $C^*$-algebra $\A$ as in Definition \ref{def:pure-mixed-state} is a disjoint union $\mathcal{P}(\A) = \bigcup_\alpha \PH_\alpha$, where $\H_\alpha$ is the Hilbert space of the irreducible GNS representation, Definition \ref{def:gns-construction}, of a state in $\PH_\alpha$. States in the same $\PH_\alpha$ are equivalent, states in different $\PH_\alpha$ are inequivalent, and each inclusion $\PH_\alpha \hookrightarrow \mathcal{P}(\A)$ is continuous.
\end{theorem}

This decomposition is proved in \cite[]{landsmanMathematicalTopicsBetween1998}. 

\begin{proposition}
	\label{prop:ph-quotient-pure-states}
	Let $\H$ be an Hilbert space, as in Definition \ref{def:hilbert} and define the space of unit norm representatives as $\mathbb{S}\H:=\{\Psi\in\H\mid(\Psi,\Psi)=1\}$. Restricting the identification in Definition \ref{def:projective-hilbert-space}, $\sim$, to $\mathbb{S}\H$ yields $[\Psi]\mapsto\tau(\Psi)$. That is a bijection $\PH\to \mathbb{S}\H/U(1)$. Under this bijection, $\PH$ is identified with the pure state space of $\mathfrak{B}_0(\H)$.
\end{proposition}

The identification with the pure states of $\mathfrak{B}_0(\H)$ follows from the correspondence between vector states and rank-one projections \cite[Section 2.5]{landsmanMathematicalTopicsBetween1998}.

\begin{definition}[Local chart of $\PH$]
	\label{def:ph-chart}
	Let $\PH$ be a projective Hilbert space as in Definition \ref{def:projective-hilbert-space}. For $\psi\in\PH$ and for $\Psi\in \mathbb{S}\H$ a representative, 
	$N_\psi:=\{\varphi\in\PH\mid(\Psi,\Phi)\neq0\}$ with $\Phi$ any
	representative of $\varphi$, set $F_\psi : N_\psi\to\H$ as 
	\begin{equation}
		F_\psi(\varphi):=\frac{\Phi}{(\Psi,\Phi)}-\Psi. \label{eq:ph-chart}
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
	\label{prop:ph-chart-homeomorphism}
	Let $F_\psi$ be the map and $N_\psi$ be the set defined in \ref{def:ph-chart}.  We show that
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $F_\psi$ is well defined,
		\item $F_\psi(\psi)=0$,
		\item the image of $F_\psi$ is $\Psi^\perp$,
		\item $F_\psi:N_\psi\to\Psi^\perp$ is a homeomorphism.
	\end{itemize}
	Hence, $(N_\psi,F_\psi,\Psi^\perp)$ is a local chart of $\PH$, as in Definition \ref{def:chart}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	$\Phi/(\Psi,\Phi)$ is invariant under $\Phi\mapsto z\Phi$, $z\neq0$, so
	$F_\psi$ is well defined, and $F_\psi(\psi)=\Psi/(\Psi,\Psi)-\Psi=0$.
	Also $F_\psi$ takes values in $\Psi^\perp$ because $(\Psi,F_\psi(\varphi))=(\Psi,\Phi)/(\Psi,\Phi)-1=0$. The map
	$\chi\mapsto\tau(\Psi+\chi)$, $\chi\in\Psi^\perp$, is a well-defined
	two-sided continuous inverse, since $\Psi+\chi\neq0$ and both maps are
	built from continuous algebraic operations on the domain
	$(\Psi,\Phi)\neq0$.
\end{proof}

Definition \ref{def:ph-chart} can be used together with its properties in Proposition \ref{prop:ph-chart-homeomorphism} to build an atlas on $\PH$, to endow it with a structure of a Hilbertian manifold. This is made explicit in the next proposition.

\begin{proposition}[Holomorphic atlas]
	\label{prop:ph-atlas}
	The collection of charts $\{(N_\psi,F_\psi,\Psi^\perp)\}_{[\Psi]\in\PH}$
	endows $\PH$ with the structure of a Hilbertian manifold, as in Definition \ref{def:hilbert-manifold}.
\end{proposition}

This proposition is proved in \cite[Construction p.72]{landsman}. The topology inherited by the manifold so far constructed is called \emph{manifold topology}. When the manifold is seen as the pure state space of a $C^*$-algebra, the weak$^*$ topology introduced in Definition \ref{def:weak_topologies} is equivalent to the manifold one.

\begin{proposition}
	\label{prop:ph-topologies-coincide}
	The manifold topology on $\PH$ coincides with the weak$^*$-topologies
	induced by $\PH\subset\mathfrak{B}_0(\H)^*$ and $\PH\subset\BH^*$, as
	per Definition~\ref{def:weak_topologies}.
\end{proposition}

The proof of this statement will not be shown here. We refer the reader to  \cite[Proposition 2.5.2]{landsman}.

\begin{corollary}
	\label{cor:pure-state-b0h-ph}
	The pure state space of $\mathfrak{B}_0(\H)$, with the weak$^*$ topology,
	is homeomorphic to $\PH$ with the manifold topology.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
	Combine Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-quotient-pure-states} with
	Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-topologies-coincide}.
\end{proof}

\noindent This is the concrete, Hilbert space incarnation of the pure
state space appearing in the $C^*$-algebraic
Definition~\ref{def:physical-system} of a quantum system.

\subsection*{Explicit Kähler structure}

Using Proposition \ref{prop:ph-chart-homeomorphism}, we explicit the construction of the Kähler manifold onto the projective Hilbert space. Let $\psi\in\PH$ and let $\Psi\in \mathbb{S}\H$ be a normalized representative, as shown in Definition \ref{prop:ph-quotient-pure-states}. By Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-chart-homeomorphism}, the holomorphic tangent space $T_\psi\PH$ is canonically isomorphic to the complex subspace $\Psi^\perp\subset\H$. To construct the Riemannian and symplectic structures, $T_\psi\PH$ must be modeled as a real tangent space. This is achieved through the realification of $\Psi^\perp$. 

The realification procedure restricts the field of scalars of $\Psi^\perp$ from $\C$ to $\R$. Consequently, the original complex scalar multiplication by the imaginary unit $i$ is reinterpreted as a real-linear endomorphism on the realified space.
\[
J:\Psi^\perp\to\Psi^\perp,\qquad JV:=iV,
\]
satisfying $J^2=-\Id$. This is an almost complex structure on $\PH$ as per Definition~\ref{def:almost-complex-structure}, integrable as per Definition~\ref{def:integrable-acs} since it is induced by the holomorphic atlas of Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-atlas}.

The Hilbert space inner product $(\cdot,\cdot)$ restricts to a
Hermitian inner product on $\Psi^\perp$. Its real and imaginary parts,
\begin{equation}
	g_0(U,V):=\Re(U,V), \qquad \omega_0(U,V):=\Im(U,V), \label{eq:g0-omega0}
\end{equation}
define a Riemannian metric $g_0$ on $\PH$, as per
Definition~\ref{def:riemannian-metric}, and a real bilinear form
$\omega_0$, with $(U,V)=g_0(U,V)+i\omega_0(U,V)$. Since scalar products are
conjugate-linear in the first argument, $(iU,iV)=(U,V)$, so
$g_0(JU,JV)=g_0(U,V)$. That agrees with condition (i) of Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold}. Evaluating instead on $(U,JV)$,
\[
(U,JV)=(U,iV)=i(U,V)=-\omega_0(U,V)+ig_0(U,V),
\]
and comparing real parts with $(U,V)=g_0(U,V)+i\omega_0(U,V)$ gives the
compatibility identity
\begin{equation}
	\omega_0(U,V)=g_0(JU,V). \label{eq:GJomega-ph}
\end{equation}
Closedness of $\omega_0$, condition (ii) of
Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold}, is verified below through
its local expression via a Kähler potential. Granting it, $(\PH,J,g_0)$ is a Kähler manifold as per Definition~\ref{def:kahler-manifold-hilbertian}. 

This triple $(\PH, J, g_0)$ constitutes the unscaled Fubini--Study structure of the projective Hilbert space. It is canonically induced by the linear inner product of $\H$ without introducing external parameters. However, $g_0$ and $\omega_0$ are dimensionless mathematical objects. To correctly encode physical dynamics and quantum statistical dispersion, this canonical structure must be uniformly scaled by a real parameter.

\subsection*{Local coordinate expression via the Kähler potential}

The function $\Phi\mapsto\|\Phi\|^2$ on $\H^*$ is the natural invariant
of the quotient defining $\PH$: for $\lambda\in\C\setminus\{0\}$,
\[
\ln\|\lambda\Phi\|^2=\ln\lambda+\ln\bar\lambda+\ln\|\Phi\|^2,
\]
and $\partial\bar\partial$ annihilates the purely holomorphic term
$\ln\lambda$ and the purely antiholomorphic term $\ln\bar\lambda$, so
$\partial\bar\partial\ln\|\lambda\Phi\|^2=\partial\bar\partial\ln\|\Phi\|^2$.
Hence $K_0:=\ln\|\Phi\|^2$ descends to a local Kähler potential, as per
Definition~\ref{def:kahler-potential}, unique up to the real part of a
holomorphic function by Remark~\ref{rem:kahler-potential-uniqueness}.

We compute Theorem~\ref{thm:metric-local-expression} on the chart
$N_\psi\simeq\Psi^\perp\simeq\C^n$ of Definition~\ref{def:ph-chart}. A
nearby state is represented by $\Phi=\Psi+\sum_{m=1}^nz^me_m$, with
$\{e_m\}$ an orthonormal basis of $\Psi^\perp$ and
$z=(z^1,\dots,z^n)\in\C^n$; since $\Psi$ is normalized and orthogonal
to each $e_m$,
\[
\|\Phi\|^2=1+\|z\|^2.
\]
The local Kähler potential is therefore $K_0(z,\bar z)=\ln(1+\|z\|^2)$,
and
\[
\frac{\partial K_0}{\partial z^j}=\frac{\bar z^j}{1+\|z\|^2},
\qquad
g_{0,j\bar k}=\frac{\partial^2K_0}{\partial z^j\partial\bar z^k}=\frac{\delta_{jk}(1+\|z\|^2)-\bar z^jz^k}{(1+\|z\|^2)^2}.
\]
At $z=0$ this gives $g_{0,j\bar k}=\delta_{jk}$, matching $g_0$ of
\eqref{eq:g0-omega0} evaluated on the orthonormal basis $\{e_m\}$. The
fundamental form is $\omega_0=i\sum_{j,k}g_{0,j\bar k}\,dz^j\wedge
d\bar z^k$, which is closed by Remark~\ref{rem:dolbeault-nilpotency},
completing the verification that $(\PH,J,g_0)$ is Kähler.

\subsection*{Curvature of the State Manifold}

We now specialize the holomorphic sectional curvature of
Definition~\ref{def:holomorphic-curvature} to the projective Hilbert
space. A direct computation will show that in the case of $\PH$ it is constant and positive. The general fact is stated by Hawley and Igusa Theorem \ref{thm:hawley-igusa} showing that projective spaces are the only possible choice for quantum mechanics.

\begin{proposition}[Curvature of the projective state space]
	\label{prop:ph-curvature-lambda}
	Let $\PH$ be the projective Hilbert space, as in Definition \ref{def:projective-hilbert-space}, endowed with the Fubini--Study metric $g_\lambda$ generated by the scaled local Kähler potential, as in Definition \ref{def:kahler-potential}, via $$K_\lambda(z, \bar{z}) := \lambda \ln(1 + \|z\|^2),$$ where $\lambda > 0$. The holomorphic sectional curvature, as in Definition \ref{def:holomorphic-curvature} of $\PH$ is strictly constant and identically equal to $2/\lambda$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	Since the unitary group acts transitively on $\PH$ by holomorphic isometries, the curvature is globally constant. QUI SERVE CITAZIONE!! it suffices to compute $H(X)$ at the coordinate origin $z=0$. 
	
	The metric components are $g_{j\bar{k}} = \partial_j \partial_{\bar{k}} K_\lambda$. Evaluating the derivatives yields:
	\begin{equation}
		g_{j\bar{k}} = \lambda \frac{\delta_{jk}(1+\|z\|^2) - \bar{z}^j z^k}{(1+\|z\|^2)^2}.
	\end{equation}
	At $z=0$, the metric evaluates to $g_{j\bar{k}}|_0 = \lambda \delta_{jk}$. The first coordinate derivatives of $g_{j\bar{k}}$ are proportional to $z$ or $\bar{z}$, thus vanishing at the origin. The non-vanishing second derivatives at $z=0$ isolate the quadratic terms in the expansion of $g_{j\bar{k}}$. Expanding $g_{j\bar{k}}$ near $z=0$:
	\begin{equation}
		g_{j\bar{k}} = \lambda \big( \delta_{jk}(1 - \|z\|^2) - \bar{z}^j z^k \big) + O(\|z\|^3) = \lambda \delta_{jk} - \lambda \sum_m \delta_{jk} z^m \bar{z}^m - \lambda z^k \bar{z}^j + O(\|z\|^3).
	\end{equation}
	Differentiating with respect to $z^m$ and $\bar{z}^l$ and evaluating at zero extracts the coefficients:
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{\partial^2 g_{j\bar{k}}}{\partial z^m \partial \bar{z}^l}\bigg|_0 = -\lambda (\delta_{jk} \delta_{ml} + \delta_{jl} \delta_{km}).
	\end{equation}
	By Definition \ref{def:holomorphic-curvature}, the curvature tensor components at $z=0$ are $R_{j\bar{k}m\bar{l}} = \lambda (\delta_{jk} \delta_{ml} + \delta_{jl} \delta_{km})$. For an arbitrary tangent vector $X = \sum v^j \partial_{z^j}$, the numerator of the holomorphic sectional curvature becomes:
	\begin{equation}
		\sum_{j,k,m,l} \lambda (\delta_{jk} \delta_{ml} + \delta_{jl} \delta_{km}) v^j \bar{v}^k v^m \bar{v}^l = \lambda \left( \left(\sum |v^j|^2\right)^2 + \left(\sum |v^j|^2\right)^2 \right) = 2\lambda \|v\|^4.
	\end{equation}
	The squared norm of the vector in the denominator is $\left(\sum g_{j\bar{k}} v^j \bar{v}^k\right)^2 = (\lambda \|v\|^2)^2 = \lambda^2 \|v\|^4$. The ratio yields:
	\begin{equation}
		H(X) = \frac{2\lambda \|v\|^4}{\lambda^2 \|v\|^4} = \frac{2}{\lambda}.
	\end{equation}
	The curvature is exactly determined by the inverse of the deformation parameter $\lambda$.
\end{proof}

This result can be generalised for any projective space. The central concept presented in the following theorem is the uniqueness, up to isomorphisms, of the projective Hilbert space formalism.

\begin{theorem}[Hawley--Igusa]
	\label{thm:hawley-igusa}
	Up to isomorphism, projective spaces are the only connected, simply connected, complete Kähler manifolds of constant, positive holomorphic sectional curvature.
\end{theorem}

The proof of this theorem is presented in \cite[Theorem 7.9]{KobayashiNomizu1969}.

\section{Dynamics}
\label{sec:dynamics}

The Kähler structure $(J,g_0)$ of Section~\ref{sec:ph-kahler} is fixed only up to an overall positive rescaling of $g_0$. In fact, replacing $g_0$ by $\lambda g_0$, for any $\lambda>0$, again satisfies Definition~\ref{def:almost-kahler-manifold}, since $J$-invariance and closedness of the fundamental form survive positive rescaling. This section fixes $\lambda$ by requiring the resulting Hamiltonian flow to reproduce the Schrödinger equation, and shows that the value obtained is exactly the one already implicit, on the algebraic side, in the convention $\qcomm{A}{B}$ fixed for the Lie--Jordan algebra of Definition~\ref{def:lie-jordan}.

We start with recalling the map $\pi:\H\setminus\{0\}\to\PH$, $x\mapsto[x]$, the quotient map defining $\PH$ as in Definition \ref{def:projective-hilbert-space}.

\begin{definition}[Mean value function]
	\label{def:mean-value}
	Given a Hilbert space $\H$, as in Definition \ref{def:hilbert}, and a bounded operator $A\in\BH$, the \emph{mean value function} of $A$ is
	\[
	\langle A\rangle : \PH \to \C, \qquad
	\langle A\rangle([x]) := \frac{(x,Ax)}{\|x\|^2}.
	\]
\end{definition}

\noindent If $A$ in Definition \ref{def:mean-value} is self-adjoint, then $\langle A\rangle$ is real valued. By Stone's Theorem \ref{thm:stone}, a self-adjoint operator $H\in\BH$ generates the one-parameter unitary group $U_t=e^{i \alpha t H}$. The physical
postulate of Schrödinger dynamics is that a state $[x]\in\PH$ evolves
along
\[
	\gamma(t):=[U_tx]=[e^{-itH/\hbar}x], \qquad t\in\R.
\]
Following this physical statement, we determine the normalization $\lambda$ of $g_0$ for which $\gamma$ is the integral curve of the Hamiltonian vector field $\Ham{\langle H\rangle}$ of Definition~\ref{def:kahlerian-function}, with respect to
$\omega=\lambda\omega_0$.

Let $v\in T_{[x]}\PH$ be a tangent vector. According to Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-chart-homeomorphism}, $v$ is uniquely represented by a vector $\eta \in x^\perp \subset \H$, where $x \in \mathbb{S}\H$ is a normalized representative of the state $[x]$.

To compute the differential $d\langle H\rangle_{[x]}(v)$, evaluate the directional derivative of the mean value function along a smooth curve $\gamma: \R \to \H\setminus\{0\}$ generating the tangent vector $\eta$. Define the linear path $\gamma(s) := x + s\eta$. Its projection to $\PH$ passes through $[x]$ at $s=0$ with velocity $v$.

The evaluation of the mean value function along this curve yields the real-valued function:
\[
	\langle H\rangle([\gamma(s)]) = \frac{(x + s\eta, H(x + s\eta))}{\|x + s\eta\|^2}.
\]
Expanding the inner products in the numerator and denominator by sesquilinearity produces:
\[
	\langle H\rangle([\gamma(s)]) = \frac{(x, Hx) + s(\eta, Hx) + s(x, H\eta) + s^2(\eta, H\eta)}{(x, x) + s(\eta, x) + s(x, \eta) + s^2(\eta, \eta)}.
\]
Imposing the normalization $(x, x) = 1$ and the orthogonality conditions $(\eta, x) = 0$ and $(x, \eta) = 0$, the denominator simplifies strictly to $1 + s^2\|\eta\|^2$. The differential is the first-order derivative with respect to the parameter $s$ evaluated at $s=0$:
\[
	d\langle H\rangle_{[x]}(v) = \left. \frac{d}{ds} \left( \frac{(x, Hx) + s(\eta, Hx) + s(x, H\eta) + s^2(\eta, H\eta)}{1 + s^2\|\eta\|^2} \right) \right|_{s=0}.
\]
Applying the quotient rule, the derivative of the denominator evaluates to zero at $s=0$, leaving only the first derivative of the numerator. The differential consequently isolates the linear coefficients
\[
	d\langle H\rangle_{[x]}(v) = (\eta, Hx) + (x, H\eta) = 2\Re(\eta, Hx).
\]
Set $\eta_H:=-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}Hx$. Since $\eta\in x^\perp$, only the
component of $\eta_H$ orthogonal to $x$ contributes to the pairings
below, so $\eta_H$ may represent the candidate vector field at $[x]$
directly. Using $\omega=\lambda\omega_0$ and $\omega_0(U,V)=\Im(U,V)$,
and conjugate-linearity of $(\cdot,\cdot)$ in the first argument,
\[
\omega_{[x]}(\eta_H,\eta)=\lambda\,\Im\big(-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}Hx,\eta\big)=\frac{\lambda}{\hbar}\,\Im\big(i(Hx,\eta)\big)=\frac{\lambda}{\hbar}\,\Re(Hx,\eta)=\frac{\lambda}{\hbar}\,\Re(\eta,Hx).
\]
Comparing with $d\langle H\rangle_{[x]}(v)=2\Re(\eta,Hx)$ shows that
$\eta_H$ represents $\Ham{\langle H\rangle}([x])$, that is,
$\iota_{\eta_H}\omega=d\langle H\rangle$, exactly when
\begin{equation}
	\lambda=2\hbar. \label{eq:lambda-fixed}
\end{equation}
\noindent Equation~\eqref{eq:lambda-fixed} fixes the free normalization of
Section~\ref{sec:ph-kahler}. From here on,
\begin{equation}
	g:=2\hbar\,g_0, \qquad \omega:=2\hbar\,\omega_0, \label{eq:physical-kahler-structure}
\end{equation}
denote the physical Fubini--Study metric and symplectic form of $\PH$,
with $I,G$ the associated musical isomorphisms of
Definition~\ref{def:musical-isomorphisms}. By linearity of the
potential in its normalization, the local Kähler potential and metric
of Section~\ref{sec:ph-kahler} rescale to
\[
K=2\hbar\ln(1+\|z\|^2), \qquad
g_{j\bar k}=2\hbar\,g_{0,j\bar k}=2\hbar\,\frac{\delta_{jk}(1+\|z\|^2)-\bar z^jz^k}{(1+\|z\|^2)^2}.
\]

This construction can be condensed in the following proposition.

\begin{proposition}[Hamiltonian flow equals Schrödinger flow]
	\label{prop:hamiltonian-schrodinger}
	Let $H\in\BH$ be self-adjoint. The Hamiltonian flow of $\langle H\rangle$ with respect to $\omega$ is
	\[
	\Phi_t([x])=[e^{-itH/\hbar}x], \qquad t\in\R,
	\]
	which satisfies the Schrödinger equation
	$i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\big(e^{-itH/\hbar}x\big)=He^{-itH/\hbar}x$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
	The value $\lambda=2\hbar$ in \eqref{eq:physical-kahler-structure} is the only normalization compatible with the Stone evolution $e^{-itH/\hbar}$. If the flow had been stated as $e^{-itH/\hbar'}$ for some $\hbar'\neq\hbar$, the computation above would have fixed $\lambda=2\hbar'$ instead.
\end{remark}

\noindent This same normalization is what identifies, via
Definition~\ref{def:kahler-bracket-products}, the geometric $*_\nu$
product at $\nu=\hbar$ with the associative product
$AB=A\circ B+\tfrac{i\hbar}{2}\{A,B\}$ of the Lie--Jordan algebra
of Definition~\ref{def:lie-jordan}. Proposition~\ref{prop:poisson-comm}
below, and Theorem~\ref{thm:lie-jordan-kahler-isomorphism}, make this
correspondence precise.

\begin{proposition}[Poisson bracket and commutator]
	\label{prop:poisson-comm}
	For self-adjoint $A,B\in\BH$,
	\[
	\poisson{\langle A\rangle}{\langle B\rangle}= \big\langle \{A,B \} \big\rangle= \big\langle\qcomm{A}{B}\big\rangle.
	\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	By Definition~\ref{def:poisson-riemann-bracket} and
	Definition~\ref{def:kahlerian-function}, $\poisson{f}{l}=df(\Ham{l})$,
	so, using the representative of $\Ham{\langle B\rangle}$ found above,
	\[
	\poisson{\langle A\rangle}{\langle B\rangle}([x])=d\langle A\rangle_{[x]}\big(\Ham{\langle B\rangle}([x])\big)=2\Re\Big(-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}Bx,Ax\Big)=-\tfrac{2}{\hbar}\Im(Bx,Ax).
	\]
	Since $A,B$ are self-adjoint,
	\[
	(x,[A,B]x)=(Ax,Bx)-(Bx,Ax)=-2i\,\Im(Bx,Ax),
	\]
	so $\big\langle\qcomm{A}{B}\big\rangle([x])=\frac{1}{i\hbar}(x,[A,B]x)=-\frac{2}{\hbar}\Im(Bx,Ax)$,
	which agrees with $\poisson{\langle A\rangle}{\langle B\rangle}([x])$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	The map $A\mapsto\langle A\rangle$ is thus a Lie algebra homomorphism
	from the self-adjoint part of $\BH$, with bracket $\qcomm{\cdot}{\cdot}$,
	to $C^\infty(\PH,\R)$, with Poisson bracket $\poisson{\cdot}{\cdot}$ with no
	extra numerical factor once the normalization of Equation~\eqref{eq:lambda-fixed}
	is in place.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Geometry of the flow}

The flow of Proposition~\ref{prop:hamiltonian-schrodinger} is generated
by the unitaries $U_t=e^{-itH/\hbar}$. Since each unitary preserves the
Hilbert space inner product, its projectivization preserves $g$ and
$\omega$, so each $\Phi_t$ is a Kähler isomorphism of $(\PH,\omega,g)$.

\begin{proposition}[Kähler isomorphisms and their generators]
	\label{prop:kahler-isomorphisms}
	Let $\H$ be an Hilbert space as in Definition \ref{def:hilbert}, and $\PH$ be its projective space as in Definition \ref{def:projective-hilbert-space}. We can state the following.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Unitary operators on $\H$ induce Kähler isomorphisms of $\PH$, and conversely every Kähler isomorphism is of this form, uniquely up to phase.
		\item Self-adjoint operators generate one-parameter groups of Kähler isomorphisms via $\Phi_t([x])=[e^{-itH/\hbar}x]$. Conversely every such group is generated by a unique $H$ up to additive constant.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	The first statement follows because unitaries preserve the inner product, hence their projectivization preserves both $\omega$ and $g$. In addition, any Kähler automorphism of $\PH$ lifts to a	unitary or antiunitary operator on $\H$ by Wigner's theorem CITAZIONE!, and restricting to continuous paths through the identity selects the unitary lifts. 
	
	For the second statement, $e^{-itH/\hbar}$ is a strongly
	continuous one-parameter unitary group by Stone's Theorem \ref{thm:stone}, so its projectivization is a continuous group of Kähler isomorphisms. Conversely, any such group lifts to a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group $U_t$, up to phases, whose self-adjoint generator $H$ satisfies $U_t=e^{-itH/\hbar}$ by Stone's Theorem \ref{thm:stone}, unique up to an additive real constant, which does not affect the projectivized flow.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	A quantum system is thus, in particular, a Hamiltonian system. A symplectic manifold $(\PH,\omega)$ together with a distinguished one-parameter group of symplectic, indeed Kähler, isomorphisms generated by the Hamiltonian function $\langle H\rangle$.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[The curvature of $\PH$ fixes $\hbar$]
	\label{cor:ph-curvature-hbar}
	The physical Kähler structure of Equations \eqref{eq:physical-kahler-structure} has constant holomorphic sectional curvature $1/\hbar$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
	The local Kähler potential $K=2\hbar\ln(1+\|z\|^2)$, obtained by rescaling as in Equation \eqref{eq:physical-kahler-structure}, is the potential $K_\lambda$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-curvature-lambda} for $\lambda=2\hbar$, whose holomorphic sectional curvature is $2/\lambda=1/\hbar$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Connecting $\hbar$ correspondences.} The simultaneous presence of the parameter $\hbar$ in the geometric $*_{\hbar}$ product and the holomorphic sectional curvature is a consequence of the Kähler compatibility requirements shown above. As we have seen, Kähler compatibility forces \(\omega = 2\hbar\,\omega_0\), giving \(g = 2\hbar\,g_0\) and \(K_{2\hbar}=2\hbar \ln(1+\|z\|^2)\). Hence, the holomorphic sectional curvature becomes \(1/\hbar\). Likewise, the Lie--Jordan algebra isomorphism requires \(\circ_{\nu}\) and \(*_{\nu}\) to be evaluated at \(\nu=\hbar\).

This specific choice is dictated by the requirement that the mean value map preserves the associative algebra structure, imposing the fundamental identity $\langle A \rangle *_{\nu} \langle B \rangle = \langle AB \rangle$. The associative operator product decomposes into its Jordan and Lie components as $AB = A \circ B + \frac{i\hbar}{2}\{A,B\}$ by Equation \eqref{eq:jordan-ass-product}. By linearity, its expectation value evaluates to $\langle AB \rangle = \langle A \circ B \rangle + \frac{i\hbar}{2}\langle \{A,B\} \rangle$. 

To reproduce this relation geometrically, one evaluates the Riemann and Poisson brackets of the observable functions $f = \langle A \rangle$ and $k = \langle B \rangle$. These brackets are constructed via the musical isomorphisms $I, G: T^*\PH \to T\PH$, which are defined implicitly by the relations $\omega(I\alpha, X) = \langle \alpha, X \rangle$ and $g(G\alpha, X) = \langle \alpha, X \rangle$. Because the physical tensors are scaled by the dynamics as $\omega = 2\hbar\omega_0$ and $g = 2\hbar g_0$, their respective inverses $I$ and $G$ inherently scale by a factor of $(2\hbar)^{-1}$. Consequently, the induced geometric brackets $\poisson{f}{k} = \langle df, I dk \rangle$ and $((f,k)) = \langle df, G dk \rangle$ absorb this exact inverse scaling. 

As established in Proposition \ref{prop:symmetric-bracket-intrinsic} and Proposition \ref{prop:poisson-comm}, this scaling yields $$\langle A \circ B \rangle = fk + \frac{\hbar}{2}((f,k))$$ and $\langle \{A,B\} \rangle = \poisson{f}{k}$. Substituting these geometric equivalents into the decomposed associative product gives $$\langle AB \rangle = fk + \frac{\hbar}{2}((f,k)) + \frac{i\hbar}{2}\poisson{f}{k}.$$ By comparing this expression with the formal definition of the geometric product, $$f *_{\nu} k := fk + \frac{\nu}{2}\big[((f,k)) + i\poisson{f}{k}\big],$$ as per Definition \ref{def:kahler-bracket-products}, the structural matching strictly forces $\nu = \hbar$. The parameter $\hbar$ in the $*_{\hbar}$ product is thus the mandatory algebraic counterpart of the inverse scaling present in $I$ and $G$, linking the non-commutative product directly to the scaled Kähler geometry.


\section{Riemannian Geometry and Statistical Distinguishability}
\label{sec:riemannian-geometry}

The Kähler structure $(\omega,g,J)$ of $\PH$ was intrinsically constructed in Section~\ref{sec:ph-kahler}. While the symplectic form $\omega$ dictates the Hamiltonian dynamics of the quantum system, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}, the Riemannian metric $g$ encodes its statistical structure. The metric captures notions of dispersion, covariance, and transition probability directly on the state manifold.

Following Definition~\ref{def:poisson-riemann-bracket} applied to the Kähler manifold $(\PH,\omega,g)$, the state space carries a symmetric Riemann bracket. For any two smooth functions $f,k \in C^\infty(\PH,\R)$, this bracket is defined by $((f,k)) = \langle df, G dk\rangle$, where $G$ is the musical isomorphism associated with the Fubini--Study metric $g$. Equivalently, by Kähler compatibility, this can be expressed as $((f,k)) = g(G df, G dk)$. We consistently employ the notation $((\cdot,\cdot))$, whether the arguments are general smooth functions on $\PH$ or mean value functions of quantum observables.

The following proposition establishes that the Jordan product of two self-adjoint operators (the symmetric part of their associative algebra) is deeply intertwined with the Riemannian structure of $\PH$, requiring no reference to the underlying linear Hilbert space.

\begin{lemma}[Metric and form via ambient representatives]
	\label{lem:g-omega-ambient}
	Let $\psi=[\Psi]\in\PH$, $\|\Psi\|=1$, and let $\eta,\eta'\in\H$ be
	arbitrary. Write $\eta_\Psi:=\eta-(\Psi,\eta)\Psi\in\Psi^\perp$ for the
	component of $\eta$ tangent to $\PH$ at $\psi$, under the identification
	$T_\psi\PH\cong\Psi^\perp$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-chart-homeomorphism}.
	Then
	\begin{equation}
		(\eta_\Psi,\eta'_\Psi)=(\eta,\eta')-(\eta,\Psi)(\Psi,\eta'), \label{eq:ambient-inner-product}
	\end{equation}
	and, by \eqref{eq:g-omega-scaled},
	\begin{equation}
		g(\eta_\Psi,\eta'_\Psi)=2\hbar\,\Re\big[(\eta,\eta')-(\eta,\Psi)(\Psi,\eta')\big], \qquad
		\omega(\eta_\Psi,\eta'_\Psi)=2\hbar\,\Im\big[(\eta,\eta')-(\eta,\Psi)(\Psi,\eta')\big]. \label{eq:g-omega-ambient}
	\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Since $\|\Psi\|=1$, $\eta_\Psi$ is the orthogonal projection of $\eta$ onto
	$\Psi^\perp$, and likewise for $\eta'_\Psi$. Writing $a:=(\Psi,\eta)$,
	$b:=(\Psi,\eta')$ and expanding by conjugate-linearity in the first
	argument,
	\begin{equation*}
		(\eta_\Psi,\eta'_\Psi)=(\eta-a\Psi,\eta'-b\Psi)
		=(\eta,\eta')-b(\eta,\Psi)-\bar a\,b+\bar a\,b
		=(\eta,\eta')-(\eta,\Psi)(\Psi,\eta'),
	\end{equation*}
	which is \eqref{eq:ambient-inner-product}; \eqref{eq:g-omega-ambient}
	follows applying \eqref{eq:g-omega-scaled}.
\end{proof}

\noindent In particular, for $A\in\BH$ self-adjoint, $x=\Psi$ normalized,
and $\eta:=-\frac i\hbar Ax$, one has $(\Psi,\eta)=-\frac
i\hbar\langle A\rangle([x])$ and $\eta_\Psi=-\frac i\hbar(A-\langle
A\rangle)x$: this recovers the vector field $\Ham{\langle A\rangle}([x])
=[-\frac i\hbar Ax]$ of Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}, the bracket there
denoting exactly the projection onto $x^\perp$ of
Lemma~\ref{lem:g-omega-ambient}.

\begin{proposition}[Intrinsic Jordan decomposition]
	\label{prop:symmetric-bracket-intrinsic}
	Let $A,K\in\BH$ be self-adjoint operators, and let $f = \langle A\rangle$ and $k = \langle K\rangle$ be their corresponding mean value functions on $\PH$. The mean value of their Jordan product $A \circ K$ is uniquely determined by the Riemann bracket on $\PH$ via the relation:
	\begin{equation}
		\langle A \circ K \rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}((f,k)) +fk.
	\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	Fix $[x]\in\PH$ and a normalized representative $\Psi=x$. By the remark
	following Lemma~\ref{lem:g-omega-ambient}, $\Ham f$ and $\Ham k$ are
	represented, under $T_{[x]}\PH\cong x^\perp$, by the projections of
	$\eta:=-\frac i\hbar Ax$ and $\eta':=-\frac i\hbar Kx$. By Kähler
	compatibility (Remark~\ref{rem:poisson-riemann-reformulation}),
	$((f,k))_\omega=g(\Ham f,\Ham k)=g(\eta_x,\eta'_x)$, and
	Lemma~\ref{lem:g-omega-ambient} applies. Using $(\eta,x)=\frac
	i\hbar\langle A\rangle([x])$, $(x,\eta')=-\frac i\hbar\langle K\rangle([x])$,
	self-adjointness of $A,K$, and $\Re(Ax,Kx)=\big(x,\tfrac12(AK+KA)x\big)
	=\langle A\circ K\rangle([x])$,
	\begin{align*}
		((f,k))_\omega([x]) &= 2\hbar\,\Re\left[\frac1{\hbar^2}(Ax,Kx)-\frac{1}{\hbar^2}\langle A\rangle([x])\langle K\rangle([x])\right]
		=\frac2\hbar\big[\langle A\circ K\rangle([x])-f([x])k([x])\big],
	\end{align*}
	which is \eqref{eq:jordan-riemann}. Equation~\eqref{eq:riemann-dispersion}
	is the case $K=A$, since $A\circ A=A^2$.
\end{proof}

\noindent The dispersions of two observable functions are controlled
jointly, through the metric and the symplectic form, by the Kähler
counterpart of the Robertson--Schrödinger uncertainty relation.
 
\begin{definition}[Dispersion]
	\label{def:dispersion}
	Let $f\in\mathcal K(\PH,\R)$ be a Kählerian function, as per Definition~\ref{def:kahlerian-function}, and let $p\in\PH$. The \emph{dispersion} of $f$ at $p$ is
	\[
	\Delta f(p) := \sqrt{\hbar/2}\,\sqrt{((f,f))(p)}.
	\]
	If $f=\langle A\rangle$ for a self-adjoint $A\in\BH$, $\Delta f(p)$ coincides with the usual quantum-mechanical standard deviation of $A$ in the state $p$, by Remark~\ref{rem:dispersion-variance} below.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	\label{rem:dispersion-variance}
	Setting $A=K$ and $f=k=\langle A\rangle$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:symmetric-bracket-intrinsic} gives $\langle A^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}((f,f))+ f^2$, that is,
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:riemann-bracket-variance}
		((f,f)) = \frac{2}{\hbar}\Big(\langle A^2 \rangle - \langle A \rangle^2\Big) = \frac{2}{\hbar}(\Delta f)^2,
	\end{equation}
	consistently with Definition~\ref{def:dispersion}. While $((f,k))$ alone does not generally represent the expectation value of a single observable, it serves as the rigorous geometric formulation of the quantum covariance between $f$ and $k$ at any given state $p \in \PH$.
\end{remark}

This purely geometric interpretation of variances and covariances paves the way to formulate the most general uncertainty relations without invoking the linear structure of $\H$.

This purely geometric interpretation of variances and covariances yields the uncertainty relations without invoking the linear structure of $\H$.

\begin{theorem}[Robertson--Schrödinger relation, geometric form]
	\label{thm:robertson-schrodinger}
	For any pair of observable functions $f,k\in\mathcal{K}(\PH,\R)$, their statistical dispersions evaluated at any state $p \in \PH$ satisfy the fundamental bound:
	\begin{equation}
		(\Delta f)(\Delta k) \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{\poisson{f}{k}^2 + ((f,k))^2}.
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Fix $[x]\in\PH$. Since $\Ham f,\Ham k\in x^\perp$ are already tangent,
	\eqref{eq:g-omega-scaled} gives directly
	\begin{equation*}
		((f,k))_\omega+i\,\poisson{f}{k}_\omega = g(\Ham f,\Ham k)+i\,\omega(\Ham f,\Ham k) = 2\hbar\,(\Ham f,\Ham k).
	\end{equation*}
	By \eqref{eq:riemann-dispersion}, $\|\Ham f\|^2=(\Ham f,\Ham
	f)=\frac1{2\hbar}((f,f))_\omega=\frac{(\Delta f)^2}{\hbar^2}$, and likewise
	for $k$. The Cauchy--Schwarz inequality on $\H$ then gives
	\begin{equation*}
		\sqrt{((f,k))_\omega^2+\poisson{f}{k}_\omega^2}=2\hbar\,|(\Ham f,\Ham k)|
		\leq 2\hbar\,\|\Ham f\|\,\|\Ham k\|=\frac2\hbar(\Delta f)(\Delta k),
	\end{equation*}
	which is the stated inequality.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	The weaker Heisenberg relation $(\Delta f)(\Delta k)\geq\frac\hbar2\left|\poisson{f}{k}_\omega\right|$
	follows dropping the Riemann bracket term. Equality in
	Theorem~\ref{thm:robertson-schrodinger} holds at $[x]\in\PH$ if and only if
	$\Ham f$ and $\Ham k$ are proportional over $\C$ there, $\Ham f=a\,\Ham
	k+b\,J\Ham k$ for some $a,b\in\R$: the extreme cases $b=0$ and $a=0$
	correspond, respectively, to the vanishing of the Poisson bracket and of
	the covariance $((f,k))_\omega$ at $[x]$.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
	For $f=\langle\sigma_x\rangle$, $k=\langle\sigma_y\rangle$ on the Bloch
	sphere, at the state $[x]=[(1,0)^T]$ one computes
	$\poisson{f}{k}=\tfrac{2}{\hbar}\langle\sigma_z\rangle=\tfrac{2}{\hbar}$
	and $((f,k))=0$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:robertson-schrodinger} gives
	$(\Delta f)(\Delta k)\geq 1$, saturated exactly at this state since
	$\langle\sigma_x\rangle=\langle\sigma_y\rangle=0$ there force
	$\Delta f=\Delta k=1$, matching the standard spin uncertainty relation.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Anandan--Aharonov relation]
	For a bounded Hamiltonian $H$ with observable function $h=\langle H\rangle$, the energy variance takes the form $(\Delta h)^2 = \frac{\hbar}{2}((h,h))$. By the Kähler compatibility outlined in Remark~\ref{rem:poisson-riemann-reformulation}, $((h,h)) = g(\Ham{h},\Ham{h})$. Thus, the energy uncertainty $\Delta h$ equals, up to a fundamental constant, the Riemannian length of the Hamiltonian vector field generating time evolution. The energy dispersion dictates the Riemannian speed at which the state traverses the projective manifold $\PH$ during unitary dynamics.
\end{example}

\noindent The Riemannian metric $g$ studied so far governs the infinitesimal
statistical structure of $\PH$. Its global role is expressed through the
distance it induces, whose extremal curves are the geodesics.

\begin{definition}[Geodesic]
	\label{def:geodesic}
	Let $(\mathcal{M},g)$ be a Riemannian manifold, as per
	Definition~\ref{def:riemannian-metric}. For a piecewise $C^1$ curve
	$\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathcal{M}$, its length is
	\begin{equation}
		L(\gamma):=\int_a^b\sqrt{g(\dot\gamma(t),\dot\gamma(t))}\,dt.
	\end{equation}
	A curve $\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathcal{M}$, parametrized proportionally to arc
	length, is a \emph{geodesic} if every $t_0\in[a,b]$ has a neighborhood
	$[t_0-\varepsilon,t_0+\varepsilon]\cap[a,b]$ on which $\gamma$ minimizes
	$L$ among piecewise $C^1$ curves with the same endpoints.
\end{definition}

\noindent Geodesics are the Riemannian analogue of straight lines, and their
lengths define a genuine distance function on $\mathcal{M}$, compatible with
its manifold topology.

\begin{definition}[Riemannian distance]
	\label{def:riemannian-distance}
	Let $(\mathcal{M},g)$ be a Riemannian manifold, as per
	Definition~\ref{def:riemannian-metric}. The Riemannian distance between
	$p,q\in\mathcal{M}$ is
	\begin{equation}
		d_g(p,q):=\inf_\gamma L(\gamma),
	\end{equation}
	where the infimum runs over piecewise $C^1$ curves
	$\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathcal{M}$ with $\gamma(a)=p$, $\gamma(b)=q$, and $L$ is
	as in Definition~\ref{def:geodesic}. If $\mathcal{M}$ is connected, $d_g$
	is a metric inducing the manifold topology, and, when attained, the
	infimum is realized by a geodesic of Definition~\ref{def:geodesic}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	\label{rem:geodesic-distance-importance}
	On $\PH$, equipped with the Fubini--Study metric $g$ of
	\eqref{eq:physical-kahler-structure}, the distance $d_g$ of
	Definition~\ref{def:riemannian-distance} is not a mere geometric
	refinement: Theorem~\ref{thm:born-rule-geodesic} below identifies it
	with the Born rule transition probability between pure states, and
	Definition~\ref{def:eigenspace-submanifold} together with
	Proposition~\ref{prop:geometric-projection} identify state vector
	reduction with a geodesic projection onto an eigenspace submanifold.
	The metric $g$ thus governs, through $d_g$, both the statistical
	distinguishability of states and the outcome of measurement.
\end{remark}

\noindent Among submanifolds of $\mathcal{M}$, those on which the ambient and
intrinsic geodesics coincide play a distinguished role: for these, the
intrinsic distance agrees with the distance measured in the ambient
manifold.

\begin{definition}[Totally geodesic submanifold]
	\label{def:totally-geodesic-submanifold}
	Let $(\mathcal{M},g)$ be a Riemannian manifold, as per
	Definition~\ref{def:riemannian-metric}, and let $N\subset\mathcal{M}$ be a
	submanifold, equipped with the induced metric $g|_N$. $N$ is
	\emph{totally geodesic} if every geodesic of $(N,g|_N)$, as per
	Definition~\ref{def:geodesic}, is also a geodesic of $(\mathcal{M},g)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	\label{rem:totally-geodesic-distance}
	If $N\subset\mathcal{M}$ is totally geodesic as per
	Definition~\ref{def:totally-geodesic-submanifold} and $\mathcal{M}$ is
	connected, the Riemannian distance $d_{g|_N}$ induced on $N$ by
	Definition~\ref{def:riemannian-distance} coincides with the restriction
	of $d_g$ to $N\times N$.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Transition Probabilities}
The same metric $g$ also has an operational meaning independent of any
fixed observable: it measures the statistical distinguishability of
nearby pure states.

\begin{theorem}[Provost--Vallee, quantum Fisher information]
	\label{thm:provost-vallee}
	Let $\theta\mapsto x(\theta)\in\H\setminus\{0\}$ be a smooth curve,
	$\psi(\theta):=[x(\theta)]\in\PH$, and $\dot x:=\partial_\theta x$. Then
	\begin{equation}
		g\left(\frac{d\psi}{d\theta},\frac{d\psi}{d\theta}\right)
		= 2\hbar\left(\frac{(\dot x,\dot x)}{(x,x)}-\frac{(x,\dot x)(\dot x,x)}{(x,x)^2}\right).
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Both sides are invariant under $x(\theta)\mapsto\lambda(\theta)x(\theta)$,
	$\lambda(\theta)\in\C\setminus\{0\}$, which leaves $\psi(\theta)$
	unchanged, so it suffices to treat a normalized curve, $\|x(\theta)\|=1$.
	At $\theta_0$, Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-chart-homeomorphism} identifies
	$d\psi/d\theta$ with the projection $\dot x(\theta_0)_{x(\theta_0)}$ of
	$\dot x(\theta_0)$ onto $x(\theta_0)^\perp$, and
	Lemma~\ref{lem:g-omega-ambient} with $\Psi=x(\theta_0)$,
	$\eta=\eta'=\dot x(\theta_0)$ gives $g(d\psi/d\theta,d\psi/d\theta)
	=2\hbar\big[(\dot x,\dot x)-|(x,\dot x)|^2\big]$ at $\theta_0$, which is the
	stated formula since $(x,x)=1$ there.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	The quantity
	\begin{equation}
		\mathcal F(\theta):=4\left(\frac{(\dot x,\dot x)}{(x,x)}-\frac{(x,\dot x)(\dot x,x)}{(x,x)^2}\right)
	\end{equation}
	is, by definition, the quantum Fisher information of the family of pure
	states $\psi(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$. Theorem~\ref{thm:provost-vallee}
	identifies it with the pulled-back metric, $g(\dot\psi,\dot\psi)
	=\frac\hbar2\mathcal F(\theta)$: the Riemannian line element of $\PH$ is,
	up to the constant $\hbar/2$, the statistical distance between
	infinitesimally separated pure states.
\end{remark}

\noindent The infinitesimal statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:provost-vallee}
integrates, along a geodesic, to a global statement relating the
distinguishability of two arbitrary pure states to their Riemannian
distance.

The metric $g$ extends beyond infinitesimal statistical dispersion to govern the macroscopic distinguishability of quantum states. Integrating the metric distance across the manifold yields the fundamental postulate of quantum measurement.

\begin{theorem}[Born rule as geodesic distance]
	\label{thm:born-rule-geodesic}
	Let $d_g : \PH \times \PH \to \R$ denote the Riemannian distance function induced by the Fubini--Study metric $g$ on $\PH$. For any two pure states $[x], [y] \in \PH$, the quantum mechanical transition probability $P([x], [y])$ satisfies
	\begin{equation}
		P([x], [y]) := \frac{|(x, y)|^2}{\|x\|^2\|y\|^2} = \cos^2\left(\frac{d_g([x], [y])}{\sqrt{2\hbar}}\right).
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}[Qubit]
	For $\H=\C^2$, the projective space $\PH$ is identified with $\C
	P^1$, the Bloch sphere of radius $\sqrt{\hbar/2}$ in the Fubini--Study
	metric $g$ of \eqref{eq:physical-kahler-structure}. Antipodal points
	on the sphere correspond to orthogonal states $[x],[y]$, for which
	$P([x],[y])=|(x,y)|^2/(\|x\|^2\|y\|^2)=0$; by
	Theorem~\ref{thm:born-rule-geodesic} this forces
	$d_g([x],[y])=\pi\sqrt{\hbar/2}$, the diameter of the sphere, matching
	the maximal geodesic distance recorded in the remark following
	Theorem~\ref{thm:hawley-igusa}. Conversely, identical states sit at
	$d_g=0$, where $P=1$, so $\cos^2$ interpolates continuously between
	certainty and mutual exclusivity as the geodesic distance ranges over
	$[0,\pi\sqrt{\hbar/2}]$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
	The metric $g$ completely determines the probabilistic structure of quantum mechanics. Orthogonal states, representing mutually exclusive measurement outcomes, correspond to points separated by the maximum geodesic distance $d_g = \pi \sqrt{\hbar/2}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Symmetry rigidity]
	The interplay between the symplectic and Riemannian structures restricts quantum observables. The infinitesimal isometries of $g$ (the Killing vector fields of $\PH$) exactly characterize the Hamiltonian vector fields of physical observable functions among all smooth scalar fields on $\PH$. This structural result is the central focus of Section~\ref{sec:observables}.
\end{remark}

\section{Observables}
\label{sec:observables}

In classical mechanics, the space of physical observables is naturally identified with the entire commutative algebra of smooth real-valued functions on the symplectic phase space. In the quantum setting, however, the rigid interplay between the symplectic structure $\omega$ and the Riemannian metric $g$ severely constrains the admissible observables. The physical observables are precisely those functions whose Hamiltonian flow respects the full Kähler geometry of the state manifold.

\begin{definition}[Observable function]
	\label{def:observable-function}
	A smooth function $f:\PH\to\R$ is an \emph{observable function} if there exists a self-adjoint operator $A\in\BH$ such that $f = \langle A\rangle$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Spectrum of an observable function]
	\label{def:observable-spectrum}
	Let $f = \langle A \rangle \in \mathcal{K}(\PH,\R)$ be an observable function associated with the self-adjoint operator $A \in \BH$. The \emph{spectrum} of $f$, denoted $\sigma(f)$, is the spectrum of the underlying operator $A$, defined algebraically as the set of values $\lambda \in \R$ such that the operator $A - \lambda \Id$ lacks a bounded two-sided inverse in $\BH$. Geometrically, the pure point spectrum of $f$ (the set of eigenvalues) coincides exactly with the set of critical values of the smooth function $f$ on $\PH$. These spectral values are attained precisely at the states where the Hamiltonian vector field vanishes, $\Ham{f} = 0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Restricting the observables to bounded self-adjoint operators is a technical convenience that entails no loss of physical generality: any realistic experiment probes a finite range of spectral values, allowing unbounded operators to be truncated. Thus, physical observables are faithfully represented by observable functions on $\PH$.
\end{remark}


By Proposition~\ref{prop:kahler-isomorphisms}, the flow generated by a self-adjoint operator $A$ via the Schrödinger equation acts on $\PH$ as a continuous one-parameter group of Kähler isomorphisms. Consequently, the Hamiltonian vector field $\Ham{f}$ of an observable function $f = \langle A \rangle$ preserves not only the symplectic form $\omega$ but also the Fubini--Study metric $g$.

\begin{definition}[Killing vector field]
	\label{def:killing-vector-field}
	Let $(\mathcal{M}, g)$ be a Riemannian manifold. A vector field $X \in \mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{M})$ is a \emph{Killing vector field} if the Lie derivative of the metric tensor $g$ with respect to $X$ vanishes identically on $\mathcal{M}$:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathcal{L}_X g = 0.
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
	On the Bloch sphere $\PH\cong\C P^1$ with the Fubini--Study metric $g$,
	the one-parameter group $[x]\mapsto[e^{-it\sigma_z/2}x]$ rotates the
	sphere about its polar axis at constant angular speed. Since rotations
	preserve the round metric, the generating vector field
	$\Ham{\langle\sigma_z\rangle}$ is Killing, illustrating
	Definition~\ref{def:killing-vector-field} concretely and confirming,
	via Theorem~\ref{thm:observable-iff-killing}, that $\langle\sigma_z\rangle$
	is an observable function.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Geometric characterization of observables]
	\label{thm:observable-iff-killing}
	A smooth function $f\in C^\infty(\PH,\R)$ is an observable function if and only if its Hamiltonian vector field $\Ham{f}$ is a Killing vector field for the Fubini--Study metric $g$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Symmetry data and finite-dimensionality]
	A fundamental property of Killing vector fields on a connected Riemannian manifold is that they are uniquely determined globally by their value and their first covariant derivative at a single point. Through the symplectic duality $\Ham{f} = I df$, this implies that an observable function is completely specified everywhere on $\PH$ by its value, its first differential, and its Hessian evaluated at any single reference state $[x] \in \PH$. The space of observables is therefore finite-dimensional whenever $\H$ is finite-dimensional, contrasting sharply with the infinite-dimensional classical algebra $C^\infty(\PH, \R)$.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:observable-iff-killing} establishes that observables are precisely the Kählerian functions (Definition~\ref{def:kahlerian-function}) of the projective Hilbert space. They act as the generators of the structural symmetries of the state manifold. This profound geometric constraint allows the non-commutative algebraic operations of $\BH$ to be translated exactly into purely geometric operations on $\PH$.

\begin{theorem}[Isomorphism of Lie--Jordan and Kähler algebras]
	\label{thm:lie-jordan-kahler-isomorphism}
	Let $(\mathcal{O},\circ,\{\cdot,\cdot\})$ be the Lie--Jordan algebra of bounded self-adjoint operators on $\H$, with $\{A,B\} := \frac{1}{i\hbar}[A,B]$ and $A \circ B := \frac{1}{2}(AB+BA)$. The expectation value map $A \mapsto \langle A \rangle$ is a strict algebraic-geometric isomorphism between $\mathcal{O}$ and the algebra of Kählerian functions $\mathcal{K}(\PH,\R)$ equipped with the geometric products $\circ_{\hbar}$ and $\poisson{\cdot}{\cdot}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	The proof proceeds by matching the algebraic operations in $\mathcal{O}$ with the geometric structures on the Kähler manifold $\PH$ evaluated on mean value functions.
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Lie structure (Symplectic form):}
		The Lie bracket $\{A,B\}$ in $\mathcal{O}$ maps to the Poisson bracket generated by the symplectic form $\omega$. By Proposition \ref{prop:poisson-comm}, substituting the scaled commutator yields:
		\begin{equation}
			\poisson{\langle A\rangle}{\langle B\rangle} = \langle \{A,B\} \rangle.
		\end{equation}
		
		\item \textbf{Jordan structure (Riemannian metric):}
		The Jordan product $A \circ B$ in $\mathcal{O}$ corresponds to the $\circ_{\hbar}$ product on $\PH$. By Proposition \ref{prop:symmetric-bracket-intrinsic} and Definition \ref{def:kahler-bracket-products}, the geometric evaluation isolates the scaled Riemann bracket:
		\begin{equation}
			\langle A \circ B \rangle = \langle A \rangle \circ_{\hbar} \langle B \rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}((\langle A \rangle, \langle B \rangle)) + \langle A \rangle \langle B \rangle.
		\end{equation}
		
		\item \textbf{Associative product (Kähler form):}
		The total associative product $AB = A \circ B + \frac{i\hbar}{2}\{A,B\}$ in the ambient algebra maps to the Kähler product $*_{\hbar}$. Applying the linearity of the expectation value map:
		\begin{align}
			\langle AB \rangle &= \langle A \circ B \rangle + \frac{i\hbar}{2}\langle \{A,B\} \rangle \nonumber \\
			&= \left( \frac{\hbar}{2}((\langle A \rangle, \langle B \rangle)) + \langle A \rangle \langle B \rangle \right) + \frac{i\hbar}{2}\poisson{\langle A\rangle}{\langle B\rangle} \nonumber \\
			&= \langle A \rangle *_{\hbar} \langle B \rangle.
		\end{align}
	\end{enumerate}
	This establishes the exact structural equivalence between the abstract Lie--Jordan algebra of operators and the intrinsically geometric operations defined on $\PH$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
	Let $A=\sigma_x$, $B=\sigma_y$ be Pauli matrices on $\H=\C^2$. Direct
	computation gives $A\circ B=\tfrac12(\sigma_x\sigma_y+\sigma_y\sigma_x)=0$
	and $\{A,B\}=\tfrac{1}{i\hbar}[\sigma_x,\sigma_y]=\tfrac{2}{\hbar}\sigma_z$,
	so that $AB=A\circ B+\tfrac{i\hbar}{2}\{A,B\}=i\sigma_z$, matching the
	familiar identity $\sigma_x\sigma_y=i\sigma_z$. Under the isomorphism
	of Theorem~\ref{thm:lie-jordan-kahler-isomorphism}, this same identity
	is recovered purely geometrically as
	$\langle\sigma_x\rangle *_{\hbar}\langle\sigma_y\rangle=\langle
	i\sigma_z\rangle$ on the Bloch sphere $\PH\cong\C P^1$, illustrating
	how the noncommutative operator product becomes the $*_{\hbar}$ product
	of Definition~\ref{def:kahler-bracket-products}.
\end{example}

\subsection*{Commutativity and Spectral Decomposition}

The algebraic-geometric isomorphism of Theorem \ref{thm:lie-jordan-kahler-isomorphism} allows the translation of the spectral theory of self-adjoint operators into purely geometric properties of Kählerian functions.

\begin{proposition}[Geometric commutativity]
	\label{prop:geometric-commutativity}
	Two observable functions $f, k \in \mathcal{K}(\PH, \R)$ represent simultaneously measurable observables if and only if their Poisson bracket vanishes identically: $\poisson{f}{k} = 0$. Geometrically, this holds if and only if their Hamiltonian vector fields commute, $[\Ham{f}, \Ham{k}] = 0$, implying that their respective isometries generate a strongly commuting Hamiltonian action on $\PH$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}
	Let $f=\langle\sigma_z\rangle$, $k=\langle\Id\rangle\equiv1$. Since
	$\{\sigma_z,\Id\}=0$, Proposition~\ref{prop:poisson-comm} gives
	$\poisson{f}{k}=0$: the two observables commute, and their Hamiltonian
	vector fields, $\Ham{f}$ and $\Ham{k}=0$, trivially commute as vector
	fields. By contrast, for $f=\langle\sigma_x\rangle$, $k=\langle\sigma_y\rangle$,
	$\poisson{f}{k}=\tfrac{2}{\hbar}\langle\sigma_z\rangle\not\equiv0$, so
	the two spin components are not simultaneously measurable, in agreement
	with Proposition~\ref{prop:geometric-commutativity}.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Geometric projector]
	\label{def:geometric-projector}
	An observable function $p \in \mathcal{K}(\PH, \R)$ is a \emph{geometric projector} if it is idempotent under the Jordan product: $p \circ_{\hbar} p = p$. By evaluating the $\circ_{\hbar}$ product, this imposes the strict metric constraint:
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{\hbar}{2}((p,p)) = p - p^2.
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
	For a two-level system $\H=\C^2$, let $\Pi$ be the rank-one orthogonal
	projector onto a fixed eigenvector, and $p:=\langle\Pi\rangle$. Since
	$\Pi^2=\Pi$, $p$ is a geometric projector in the sense of
	Definition~\ref{def:geometric-projector}. On the corresponding
	eigenspace submanifold $\mathbb{P}E_1\subset\PH$, a single point in
	this case, $p=1$ and $((p,p))=0$; on the orthogonal eigenspace
	$\mathbb{P}E_0$, $p=0$ and again $((p,p))=0$. At every other state
	$[x]\in\PH$, $p([x])\in(0,1)$ strictly, and the metric constraint
	$\tfrac{\hbar}{2}((p,p))=p-p^2$ forces $((p,p))>0$ there, in agreement
	with the remark following Definition~\ref{def:geometric-projector}.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
	The metric constraint forces $p([x]) \in [0,1]$ for all $[x] \in \PH$, since the Riemann bracket $((p,p)) = g(\Ham{p}, \Ham{p})$ is strictly non-negative. The zero-variance loci where $((p,p)) = 0$ correspond precisely to the regions where the function $p$ attains the absolute values $1$ or $0$. These loci form exactly the totally geodesic eigenspace submanifolds associated with the projector.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Geometric Spectral Theorem]
	\label{thm:geometric-spectral}
	Let $f \in \mathcal{K}(\PH, \R)$ be an observable function possessing a finite discrete spectrum $\sigma(f) = \{a_1, \dots, a_m\}$. There exists a unique set of geometric projectors $\{p_1, \dots, p_m\} \subset \mathcal{K}(\PH, \R)$ such that:
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item $\sum_{j=1}^m p_j = 1$ (Resolution of the identity).
		\item $p_j \circ_{\hbar} p_k = \delta_{jk} p_j$ (Mutual orthogonality).
		\item $f = \sum_{j=1}^m a_j p_j$ (Spectral decomposition).
	\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\subsection*{State Vector Reduction}

\begin{definition}[Eigenspace submanifold]
	\label{def:eigenspace-submanifold}
	Let $A \in \BH$ be a self-adjoint operator with discrete spectrum $\sigma(A)$. For each $a \in \sigma(A)$, let $E_a \subset \H$ be the corresponding closed eigenspace. The projectivization $\mathbb{P}E_a \subset \PH$ is a closed, totally geodesic, as per Definition~\ref{def:totally-geodesic-submanifold}, Kähler submanifold of $\PH$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
	For $A=\sigma_z$ on $\H=\C^2$, the eigenspaces $E_{\pm1}$ are the two
	one-dimensional subspaces spanned by the poles of the Bloch sphere, so
	$\mathbb{P}E_{\pm1}$ are single points, trivially totally geodesic as
	per Definition~\ref{def:eigenspace-submanifold}. For a degenerate
	operator such as $A=\sigma_z\oplus\sigma_z$ on $\H=\C^2\otimes\C^2$
	restricted to a two-dimensional eigenspace, $\mathbb{P}E_a$ is instead
	a great circle of the higher-dimensional $\PH$, again totally geodesic,
	illustrating that geometric multiplicity controls the dimension of the
	submanifold, not merely its existence.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Physical meaning of totally geodesic submanifolds]
	\label{rem:totally-geodesic-physical}
	By Remark~\ref{rem:totally-geodesic-distance}, the Riemannian distance measured intrinsically within the submanifold $\mathbb{P}E_a$ matches the distance measured in the ambient space $\PH$. Physically, this guarantees that any quantum superposition of two degenerate eigenstates belonging to $\mathbb{P}E_a$ yields another valid eigenstate lying strictly within the same submanifold. The linear structure of the degenerate eigenspace is therefore preserved globally without geometric distortion.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Geometric measurement projection]
	\label{prop:geometric-projection}
	Let $p \in \PH$ be a pre-measurement state and $f = \langle A \rangle$ an observable function. Upon a single experimental measurement of $f$ yielding a single definite value $a \in \sigma(A)$, the state reduction $p \mapsto p_a$ is the unique geometric projection onto the eigenspace submanifold $\mathbb{P}E_a$ minimizing the Riemannian distance:
	\begin{equation}
		d_g(p, p_a) = \inf_{q \in \mathbb{P}E_a} d_g(p, q).
	\end{equation}
	The resulting reduced state $p_a$ is a single, deterministic physical state corresponding to the single obtained value, strictly distinct from a statistical sum or ensemble average.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Born rule for state reduction]
	\label{thm:born-rule-reduction}
	Under the geometric measurement projection $p \mapsto p_a$ defined in Proposition \ref{prop:geometric-projection}, the quantum mechanical probability $P(a|p)$ of obtaining the specific outcome $a \in \sigma(A)$ is strictly determined by the minimized Riemannian distance between the pre-measurement state and the target eigenspace submanifold:
	\begin{equation}
		P(a|p) = \cos^2\left(\frac{d_g(p, p_a)}{\sqrt{2\hbar}}\right).
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Measurement vs. Unitary Dynamics]
	\label{rem:measurement-vs-unitary}
	The projection mapping $p \mapsto p_a$ constitutes a discontinuous, non-invertible geometric transformation. Unlike the Hamiltonian flow generated by an observable (Theorem \ref{thm:observable-iff-killing}), which acts via Kähler isometries and preserves the full geometric structure of $\PH$, the measurement projection collapses the symplectic form and strictly decreases the distance between non-orthogonal states. This geometric discontinuity rigorously encodes the difference between the deterministic unitary evolution and the quantum state reduction process.
\end{remark}

\section{Quantum Symmetries and the Momentum Map}
\label{sec:quantum-momentum-map}

In the classical Hamiltonian framework, continuous symmetries are defined by symplectic group actions, with the associated conserved quantities encoded algebraically in the momentum map, as established in Definition \ref{def:momentum_mapping}. Translating this apparatus into Geometric Quantum Mechanics requires enforcing the rigid constraints of the Kähler structure of the projective Hilbert space $\PH$.

While a classical symmetry only requires the preservation of the symplectic form $\omega$, a physical quantum symmetry must act via Kähler isometries, thereby preserving the fundamental triad $(\omega, g, J)$. By Wigner's theorem, continuous groups of such isometries are induced by strongly continuous unitary representations on the underlying Hilbert space $\H$.

\begin{proposition}[Quantum momentum map]
	\label{prop:quantum-momentum-map}
	Let $G \subseteq U(\H)$ be a connected Lie group acting on $\PH$ via projective unitary transformations. Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be its Lie algebra, consisting of skew-adjoint operators on $\H$. The induced action on $\PH$ is strongly Hamiltonian. The unique equivariant momentum map $\mu: \PH \to \mathfrak{g}^*$, satisfying $d\langle \mu, \xi \rangle = \iota_{\xi_{\PH}} \omega$ as per Definition \ref{def:momentum_mapping}, is given pointwise by
	\begin{equation}
		\langle \mu([x]), \xi \rangle = \langle i\hbar \xi \rangle([x]) = \frac{(x, i\hbar \xi x)}{\|x\|^2},
	\end{equation}
	where $i\hbar \xi$ is the corresponding self-adjoint generator in $\BH$, and $\langle \cdot \rangle$ is the mean value function.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}
	Let $G=U(1)$ act on $\H$ by $\Psi\mapsto e^{i\theta}\Psi$, with Lie
	algebra generator $\xi=i\cdot\Id$. Since this action is trivial on
	$\PH$ (it fixes every ray $[\Psi]$), the induced vector field
	$\xi_{\PH}$ vanishes identically, and the momentum map of
	Proposition~\ref{prop:quantum-momentum-map} reduces to the constant
	$\langle\mu([x]),\xi\rangle=\langle i\hbar\xi\rangle([x])=-\hbar$,
	consistent with the overall phase of $\Psi$ carrying no physical
	content on the projective state space.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Topological exactness and structural rigidity]
	\label{rem:momentum-differences}
	The transition from classical phase spaces to $\PH$ introduces two fundamental geometric distinctions regarding the momentum map.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \emph{Non-exactness of the symplectic form:} Classical momentum maps are frequently constructed via the exactness of the symplectic form $\omega = -d\theta$, typical of cotangent bundles $T^*Q$. Conversely, the Fubini--Study form $\omega$ on $\PH$ is closed but not exact (for $\dim \H < \infty$, $\PH$ is compact, strictly prohibiting exact symplectic forms). The existence of the global momentum map $\mu$ on $\PH$ relies instead on the topological fact that $\PH$ is simply connected.
		\item \emph{Metric constraints:} In classical mechanics, the component functions $\langle \mu, \xi \rangle$ are merely smooth real functions generating symplectic vector fields. In GQM, because the flow of $\xi_{\PH}$ preserves the Riemannian metric $g$, Theorem \ref{thm:observable-iff-killing} guarantees that the components of the momentum map are strictly Kählerian functions (Killing vector fields). The classical observable corresponding to a continuous symmetry direction $\xi$ maps identically to the expectation value of the quantum observable $i\hbar\xi$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

The classical Geometric Noether Theorem, Theorem \ref{thm:geometric_noether_iff}, translates directly into the quantum formalism. The structure of $\PH$ uniquely identifies the vanishing of the classical Poisson bracket with the vanishing of the quantum commutator.

\begin{theorem}[Quantum Noether Theorem]
	\label{thm:quantum-noether}
	Let $H \in \BH$ be a self-adjoint Hamiltonian operator governing the dynamics on $\PH$ via $X_{\langle H \rangle}$. Let $G \subseteq U(\H)$ be a symmetry group acting on $\PH$ with momentum map $\mu: \PH \to \mathfrak{g}^*$. The momentum map is an integral of motion, meaning $\mu(\Phi_t([x])) = \mu([x])$ for all $t \in \R$, if and only if the algebra of generators strictly commutes with the Hamiltonian:
	\begin{equation}
		[H, i\hbar \xi] = 0 \qquad \forall \xi \in \mathfrak{g}.
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	By Theorem \ref{thm:geometric_noether_iff}, $\mu$ is conserved along the flow of $X_{\langle H \rangle}$ if and only if the Poisson bracket vanishes: $\{ \langle H \rangle, \langle \mu, \xi \rangle \} = 0$ for all $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$. Substituting the explicit form of the quantum momentum map from Proposition \ref{prop:quantum-momentum-map}, the condition becomes $\{ \langle H \rangle, \langle i\hbar \xi \rangle \} = 0$. Applying the algebraic-geometric correspondence of Proposition \ref{prop:correspondence}, this symplectic bracket maps exactly to the mean value of the operator commutator:
	\begin{equation}
		\{ \langle H \rangle, \langle i\hbar \xi \rangle \} = \left\langle \frac{1}{i\hbar} [H, i\hbar \xi] \right\rangle.
	\end{equation}
	The function $\langle \frac{1}{i\hbar} [H, i\hbar \xi] \rangle$ vanishes identically on $\PH$ if and only if the corresponding operator $[H, i\hbar \xi]$ is the zero operator in $\BH$.
\end{proof}

\section{Algebraic-Geometric Unification and Axiomatics}
\label{sec:unification}

The preceding chapter produced a reconstruction of quantum mechanics from primitive data wholly different from those used here. Starting from the operational notions of preparations and tests, Section~\ref{sec:intro-alg-quant} recovered a unital $C^*$-algebra $\A$ together with a separating state space $S$, the pair fixed as the definition of a quantum system in Definition~\ref{def:physical-system}. The GNS construction of Theorem~\ref{thm:gns}, and its faithful extension in the Gelfand--Naimark Theorem~\ref{thm:gelfand-naimark}, turned this algebraic datum into a genuine Hilbert-space representation, matched article by article against the Dirac--von Neumann axioms of Definition~\ref{def:concl-dvn-axioms}. Starting instead from the linear structure of a fixed Hilbert space $\H$, the present chapter built the Kähler manifold $\PH$ and showed that its symplectic form $\omega$, its Riemannian metric $g$, and the brackets and products of Definitions~\ref{def:poisson-riemann-bracket} and~\ref{def:kahler-bracket-products} reproduce, on mean value functions, the Lie--Jordan operations of the bounded self-adjoint operators on $\H$. This section makes precise how the two reconstructions attach to one another, states what carries over from one to the other and what does not, and collects the dynamical and measurement-theoretic results already proved in this chapter into a single geometric axiomatization, alongside the algebraic axioms it inherits rather than replaces.

\subsection*{From the GNS representation to the geometry of pure states}

The GNS Theorem~\ref{thm:gns} attaches to every state $\omega$ on $\A$ a Hilbert space $\H_\omega$, a representation $\pi_\omega$, and a cyclic vector $\Psi_\omega$ reproducing $\omega$ as a matrix element. Nothing in this construction singles out $\PH_\omega$: it fixes a point $\Psi_\omega\in\H_\omega$, not a manifold, and neither the associative product on $\A$ nor its GNS representation carries a symplectic form, a Riemannian metric, or a notion of Hamiltonian flow. These are supplied only once GNS is combined with purity.

\begin{remark}[Vector states and cyclic vectors]
	\label{rem:algebraic-geometric-states}
	The state space $S$, defined operationally via positive linear functionals on the $C^*$-algebra $\A$, is geometrically realized through the GNS construction. For any pure state $\omega \in \mathcal{P}(\A)$, the GNS representation yields a Hilbert space $\H_\omega$ and a cyclic vector $\Psi_\omega \in \H_\omega$. The projective ray $[\Psi_\omega] \in \PH_\omega$ is the unique geometric point corresponding to the algebraic state $\omega$. The evaluation of the state functional maps identically to the mean value function:
	\begin{equation}
		\omega(A) = \frac{(\Psi_\omega, A \Psi_\omega)}{\|\Psi_\omega\|^2} = \langle A \rangle([\Psi_\omega]), \qquad \forall A \in \A.
	\end{equation}
\end{remark}

\noindent By Proposition~\ref{prop:irreducible-iff-pure}, $\pi_\omega$ is irreducible exactly when $\omega$ is pure, and Theorem~\ref{thm:pure-state-decomposition} extends Remark~\ref{rem:algebraic-geometric-states} from a single state to the whole pure state space:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{P}(\A) = \bigcup_\alpha \PH_\alpha,
\end{equation}
a disjoint union indexed by the unitarily inequivalent irreducible representations of $\A$, with $\PH_\alpha$ the projective space of the corresponding GNS Hilbert space $\H_\alpha$: states lying in the same $\PH_\alpha$ correspond to equivalent representations, states in different $\PH_\alpha$ to inequivalent ones. Theorem~\ref{thm:pure-state-decomposition} exhibits $\mathcal P(\A)$ as stratified by superselection sector; it does not by itself supply a single manifold $\PH$.

\noindent The construction of Section~\ref{sec:ph-kahler} onward specializes this decomposition to a single sector. Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-quotient-pure-states} shows directly that $\PH$, for a fixed Hilbert space $\H$, is in bijection with the whole pure state space $\mathcal P(\mathfrak B_0(\H))$ of the compact operators on $\H$; since Theorem~\ref{thm:pure-state-decomposition} partitions $\mathcal P(\mathfrak B_0(\H))$ into sectors indexed by irreducible representations, this bijection forces the partition to consist of the single term $\PH_\alpha=\PH$ for $\A=\mathfrak B_0(\H)$. Every construction from Section~\ref{sec:ph-kahler} to Section~\ref{sec:quantum-momentum-map} takes place inside this one sector. An algebra $\A$ with several such sectors would instead require one Kähler manifold $\PH_\alpha$ per sector, glued only at the level of the disjoint union $\mathcal P(\A)$ and not at the level of any single symplectic or Riemannian structure; this case falls outside the present formulation.

\subsection*{The Lie--Jordan algebra and the Kähler algebra of observables}

Once a single sector $\PH$ is fixed, the mean value map $A\mapsto\langle A\rangle$ turns algebraic operations on $\BH$ into differential-geometric operations on $\PH$. Proposition~\ref{prop:poisson-comm} identifies the geometric Poisson bracket of Definition~\ref{def:poisson-riemann-bracket} with the mean value of the rescaled commutator, $\poisson{\langle A\rangle}{\langle B\rangle}_\omega=\langle\{A,B\}\rangle$ for $\{A,B\}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[A,B]$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:symmetric-bracket-intrinsic} identifies the Riemann bracket with the Jordan anticommutator, $\big\langle\tfrac12\{A,K\}_+\big\rangle=\tfrac\hbar2((\langle A\rangle,\langle K\rangle))_\omega+\langle A\rangle\langle K\rangle$. Theorem~\ref{thm:lie-jordan-kahler-isomorphism} assembles the two into a single statement: $A\mapsto\langle A\rangle$ is an isomorphism of the Lie--Jordan algebra $\mathcal O$ of bounded self-adjoint operators on $\H$, with Jordan product $\circ$ and bracket $\{A,B\}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[A,B]$, onto the algebra of Kählerian functions $\mathcal K(\PH,\R)$ equipped with the geometric products $\circ_{\hbar}$ and $\poisson{\cdot}{\cdot}_\omega$. Theorem~\ref{thm:observable-iff-killing} identifies this target algebra exactly with the observable functions of Definition~\ref{def:observable-function}: a smooth $f\in C^\infty(\PH,\R)$ equals $\langle A\rangle$ for some self-adjoint $A\in\BH$ if and only if its Hamiltonian vector field $\Ham{f}$ is a Killing field for the Fubini--Study metric $g$, equivalently, if and only if $f$ is Kählerian in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:kahlerian-function}. The Poisson bracket by itself already closes on the strictly larger algebra $C^\infty(\PH,\R)$; it is the further, metric-preserving requirement built into the Kähler, rather than merely symplectic, compatibility of $g$ and $\omega$ that cuts this algebra down to exactly the mean value functions of $\BH$.

The correspondence extends from the Jordan and Lie parts of the product to the full associative product once the mean value map is complexified.

\begin{definition}[Geometric $C^*$-norm]
	\label{def:geometric-norm}
	For $f\in C^\infty(\PH,\C)$ such that the supremum below is finite, define the geometric norm
	\begin{equation}
		\|f\|_{\hbar} := \sqrt{ \sup_{[x]\in\PH}(\bar f *_{\hbar} f)([x])}.
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Isomorphism of $C^*$-algebras]
	\label{thm:cstar-isomorphism}
	Let $\A \subseteq \BH$ be a $C^*$-algebra, and let $\mathcal{K}_{\A}(\PH,\C)$ denote the space of complexified mean value functions $A\mapsto\langle A\rangle$ for all $A\in\A$. Equipped with the $*_{\hbar}$ product, pointwise complex conjugation, and the norm $\Vert\cdot\Vert_{\hbar}$, $\mathcal{K}_{\A}(\PH,\C)$ is a $C^*$-algebra. Furthermore, the map $A\mapsto\langle A\rangle$ is an isometric isomorphism of $C^*$-algebras between $\A$ and $\mathcal{K}_{\A}(\PH,\C)$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent Together with Theorem~\ref{thm:lie-jordan-kahler-isomorphism}, Theorem~\ref{thm:cstar-isomorphism} shows that the four geometric products of Definition~\ref{def:kahler-bracket-products}, the Riemann bracket, the Poisson bracket, the $\circ_\nu$ product, and the $*_\nu$ product, are not independent geometric conveniences: restricted to the image of a $C^*$-subalgebra $\A\subseteq\BH$ under the mean value map, they are the Jordan product, the rescaled commutator, and the associative product of $\A$ itself, rewritten in the geometric data supplied by $\PH$.

\subsection*{Dynamics: from unitary evolution to Kähler automorphisms}

None of the material recalled so far constrains time evolution. The Lie--Jordan structure of $\mathcal O$ and its Kähler counterpart on $\mathcal K(\PH,\R)$ are static algebraic data; a dynamics has to be added on top of them. Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} added it at the level of the linear Hilbert space, taking the physical evolution of a state vector under a self-adjoint Hamiltonian $H\in\BH$ to be $\dot x=-\tfrac i\hbar Hx$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:hamiltonian-schrodinger} showed by direct computation that the projected curve $\gamma(t)=[e^{-itH/\hbar}x_0]$ is exactly the integral curve of the Hamiltonian vector field $\Ham{\langle H\rangle}$ with respect to $\omega$. Symplectic geometry alone would not force this identification: any smooth function on $\PH$ generates a Hamiltonian flow with respect to $\omega$, and nothing about $(\PH,\omega)$ by itself privileges the flows generated by Kählerian functions over those generated by an arbitrary element of $C^\infty(\PH,\R)$. What Proposition~\ref{prop:kahler-isomorphisms} adds is the converse: a continuous one-parameter group of transformations of $\PH$ preserving the full Kähler triad $(\omega,g,J)$, and not merely $\omega$, is necessarily of Schrödinger form, generated by a self-adjoint operator unique up to an additive real constant, via Wigner's theorem and Stone's theorem. Requiring dynamics to be generated by an observable function, equivalently by a Kählerian one, equivalently by a Killing field for $g$, is therefore what singles out physically admissible time evolution among the far larger class of symplectic Hamiltonian flows on $\PH$; this requirement, not the symplectic structure by itself, is the geometric counterpart of postulating unitary evolution at the Hilbert-space level. Theorem~\ref{thm:quantum-noether} then reads the vanishing of the Poisson bracket $\poisson{\langle H\rangle}{\langle\mu,\xi\rangle}_\omega$, equivalently of the commutator $[H,i\hbar\xi]$, as the conservation of the momentum map component $\langle\mu,\xi\rangle$ along this flow, so that the algebraic and geometric statements of Noether's theorem coincide term by term through the isomorphism of Theorem~\ref{thm:lie-jordan-kahler-isomorphism}.

\subsection*{Measurement: spectral values, the Born rule, and state reduction}

The algebraic reconstruction already attaches a probabilistic reading to the spectrum of an observable: for a normal $A\in\A$, every $\lambda\in\sigma(A)$ is attained as the expectation value of some state, dispersion-free exactly at states supported on the corresponding eigenspace. The geometric counterpart of this fact is recorded below.

\begin{remark}[Spectral attainment and totally geodesic submanifolds]
	\label{rem:spectrum-submanifolds}
	The algebraic attainment of spectral values for normal elements establishes that for every $\lambda \in \sigma(A)$ there exists a state yielding $\lambda$ with zero variance. This correlates strictly with the metric structure of $\PH$. Geometrically, the condition $\Delta \langle A \rangle = 0$ implies the Riemann bracket vanishes, $((\langle A \rangle, \langle A \rangle))_\omega = 0$. This occurs if and only if the Hamiltonian vector field $X_{\langle A \rangle}$ vanishes at the state $[x]$. The set of zeros of the Killing vector field $X_{\langle A \rangle}$ forms exactly the disjoint union of the totally geodesic eigenspace submanifolds $\mathbb{P}E_\lambda$.
\end{remark}

\noindent Beyond the location of dispersion-free states, two further rules of the standard formalism are external to the mathematics of $(\PH,\omega,g)$ as such, and are reformulated, rather than derived, by the results of Section~\ref{sec:riemannian-geometry} and Section~\ref{sec:observables}. Nothing in the definition of the Kähler structure forces the quantity $P([x],[y]):=|(x,y)|^2/(\|x\|^2\|y\|^2)$ to be read as a transition probability; once this reading is adopted, Theorem~\ref{thm:born-rule-geodesic} is the mathematical fact that $P([x],[y])=\cos^2\!\big(d_g([x],[y])/\sqrt{2\hbar}\big)$, so the Born rule becomes a statement purely about the Riemannian distance $d_g$ of the Fubini--Study metric. Likewise, that a measurement of $A$ yielding $a\in\sigma(A)$ leaves the system in the eigenspace of $a$ is itself a postulate of the standard formalism; Proposition~\ref{prop:geometric-projection} is the theorem that the resulting reduced state $p_a$ is the unique point of $\mathbb{P}E_a$, with $\mathbb{P}E_a$ as in Definition~\ref{def:eigenspace-submanifold}, minimizing the Riemannian distance $d_g(p,\cdot)$ from the pre-measurement state $p$. In both cases the physical content, that a specific algebraic quantity is a probability and that collapse selects a specific state, is inherited unchanged from the standard formulation; the geometry contributes an intrinsic, coordinate-free description of what these rules amount to on $\PH$, stated without reference back to $\H$.

\begin{remark}[Topological completeness and continuous spectra]
	\label{rem:completeness-spectra}
	The necessity of topological completion for pre-Hilbert spaces and the bounded extension of operators dictate the metric limits of $\PH$. For observables with continuous spectra, exact eigenvectors do not exist within the physical Hilbert space $\H$. Consequently, the geometric measurement projection cannot target a closed submanifold within $\PH$. The rigorous geometric treatment of continuous spectra necessitates extending $\PH$ to a projective rigged Hilbert space, mirroring the algebraic extension of bounded operators to distributions.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{The axiomatic system}

By the Hawley--Igusa Theorem~\ref{thm:hawley-igusa}, a connected, simply connected, complete Kähler manifold of constant positive holomorphic sectional curvature is, up to isomorphism, a complex projective space. By Corollary~\ref{cor:ph-curvature-hbar}, $\PH$ realizes exactly this curvature, at the value $1/\hbar$, so fixing $\hbar$ pins down the
manifold itself and not merely its local type, which is what makes it consistent to take the Kähler structure, rather than the ambient Hilbert space $\H$, as the primitive datum of the following definition, already introduced in this chapter.

\begin{definition}[Schrödinger quantum system]
	\label{def:schrodinger-quantum-system}
	A \emph{Schrödinger quantum system} is a quadruple $(\mathcal{M},J,g,(\Phi_t)_{t\in\R})$, where $(\mathcal{M},J,g)$ is a connected, simply connected, complete Kähler manifold of constant holomorphic sectional curvature $1/\hbar$, and $(\Phi_t)_{t\in\R}$ is a continuous one-parameter group of Kähler isomorphisms of $(\mathcal{M},J,g)$.
\end{definition}

\noindent Definition~\ref{def:schrodinger-quantum-system} is purely differential-geometric, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:hawley-igusa} together with Proposition~\ref{prop:kahler-isomorphisms} it already forces $\mathcal M\cong\PH$ for some Hilbert space $\H$, and $\Phi_t([x])=[e^{-itH/\hbar}x]$ for some self-adjoint $H$ unique up to an additive constant, recovering the Hilbert-space data this chapter started from. It is not, however, a self-contained substitute for Definition~\ref{def:physical-system}, and the two should be read together rather than as competing formulations. What Definition~\ref{def:schrodinger-quantum-system} packages geometrically is: the kinematic content of Definition~\ref{def:physical-system}, restricted through Theorem~\ref{thm:pure-state-decomposition} and Proposition~\ref{prop:ph-quotient-pure-states} to a single sector; the observable algebra, recovered as $\mathcal K(\mathcal M,\R)\cong\mathcal O$ through Theorems~\ref{thm:observable-iff-killing} and~\ref{thm:lie-jordan-kahler-isomorphism}; and the dynamics, recovered as the requirement, argued in the previous subsection, that $(\Phi_t)$ preserve the full Kähler triad and not merely $\omega$. It packages nothing about measurement: the Born rule and the state reduction rule remain physical postulates external to $(\mathcal M,J,g,(\Phi_t))$, merely rewritten intrinsically by Theorem~\ref{thm:born-rule-geodesic} and Proposition~\ref{prop:geometric-projection}, and have to be adjoined to Definition~\ref{def:schrodinger-quantum-system} separately to obtain a complete physical theory rather than a geometric model of its kinematics and dynamics alone. What the geometric formulation adds to Definition~\ref{def:physical-system} is accordingly not a different physical content but a single object, the Kähler manifold $\mathcal M$, from which the algebra of observables, their Poisson and Jordan structure, the dynamics, and both measurement rules are all read off from one Riemannian and symplectic structure, in place of the separate algebraic, representation-theoretic, and spectral data of Definition~\ref{def:physical-system}, Theorem~\ref{thm:gns}, and the attainment of spectral values on which Remark~\ref{rem:spectrum-submanifolds} was based.

\begin{remark}[Density operators and stratified spaces]
	\label{rem:density-operators-stratified}
	The geometric formulation on the projective Hilbert space restricts strictly to pure states. The space of mixed states, consisting of positive trace-class operators with unit trace on $\H$, does not possess the structure of a smooth global manifold. It constitutes a stratified space, where each stratum is a smooth manifold composed of density operators of a fixed constant rank. The Poisson bracket, the Riemann bracket, and the Kähler products extend differentiably only within individual strata. The algebraic isomorphism established in Theorem~\ref{thm:cstar-isomorphism} breaks down across topological boundaries separating strata of differing dimensions.
\end{remark}

\section{Example III: Dynamics and Symmetries on the Bloch Sphere}
\label{sec:ex-three}

\begin{example}[The Bloch Sphere as a Kähler Manifold]
	\label{ex:bloch-sphere-kahler}
	Consider the one-dimensional complex projective space $\mathbb{C}P^1$, representing the state manifold of a two-level quantum system. This space provides a rigorous finite-dimensional application of the Kählerian framework.
	
	\subsection*{1. Holomorphic Atlas}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Let $[z_0 : z_1]$ denote the homogeneous coordinates of $\mathbb{C}P^1$.
		\item Define the standard affine chart $U_0 := \{[z_0 : z_1] \mid z_0 \neq 0\}$.
		\item The local holomorphic coordinate on $U_0$ is defined as the affine ratio $w := \frac{z_1}{z_0} \in \mathbb{C}$.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection*{2. Fubini-Study Kähler Potential}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item A state vector in the underlying Hilbert space $\mathbb{C}^2$ corresponding to the coordinate $w$ is represented by the unnormalized vector $\Phi = (1, w)^T$.
		\item The squared norm evaluates to $\|\Phi\|^2 = 1 + |w|^2 = 1 + w\bar{w}$.
		\item The local Kähler potential on the chart $U_0$, scaled by the physical constant $2\hbar$, is precisely $K(w, \bar{w}) = 2\hbar \ln(1 + w\bar{w})$.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection*{3. Metric Tensor Derivation}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The components of the Hermitian metric tensor $g$ are obtained through the mixed Dolbeault derivatives of the Kähler potential.
		\item The first holomorphic derivative evaluates to $\partial_w K = 2\hbar \frac{\bar{w}}{1 + w\bar{w}}$.
		\item The second mixed derivative yields the positive definite metric component:
		\begin{equation}
			g_{w\bar{w}} = \partial_{\bar{w}} \partial_w K = 2\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial \bar{w}} \left( \frac{\bar{w}}{1 + w\bar{w}} \right) = \frac{2\hbar}{(1 + |w|^2)^2}.
		\end{equation}
		\item The invariant Riemannian line element is $ds^2 = g_{w\bar{w}} dw \otimes d\bar{w} = \frac{2\hbar}{(1 + |w|^2)^2} (dw \otimes d\bar{w})$.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection*{4. Symplectic Form and Integrability}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The fundamental 2-form $\omega$ is constructed directly from the metric components:
		\begin{equation}
			\omega = i g_{w\bar{w}} dw \wedge d\bar{w} = i \frac{2\hbar}{(1 + |w|^2)^2} dw \wedge d\bar{w}.
		\end{equation}
		\item The integrability condition defining an almost Kähler manifold requires the fundamental form to be closed, $d\omega = 0$.
		\item Since $\mathbb{C}P^1$ is a manifold of real dimension 2 (complex dimension 1), $\omega$ is a top-degree form, forcing $d\omega = 0$ identically.
	\end{itemize}
	The almost complex structure is integrable by construction, and $\omega$ is closed and non-degenerate. Consequently, the triple $(\mathbb{C}P^1, J, g)$ rigorously satisfies all conditions of a Kähler manifold.
\end{example}

We investigate time evolution and symmetries on the state space of a two-level quantum system. Restricting the space of density matrices to the boundary $|\mathbf{r}|=1$ yields the ray space $\mathbb{P}\C^2 \cong \mathbb{S}^2$, identified via Definition~\ref{def:projective-hilbert-space} with $\mathbb{P}\mathcal{H}$ for $\mathcal{H}=\C^2$. This manifold carries the Kähler structure $(J,g)$ introduced in Section~\ref{sec:ph-kahler}. The Bloch coordinates $\mathbf{r}$ coincide, componentwise, with the mean value functions $r_i := \langle\sigma_i\rangle$ of Definition~\ref{def:mean-value}. These functions serve as global coordinates on $\mathbb{P}\mathcal{H}(\C^2)$. Since mixed states do not form a manifold, only this boundary sphere admits a symplectic form $\omega$. We evaluate the Poisson and Riemann brackets between the mean value functions $r_i$ to verify the uncertainty relations.

By Proposition~\ref{prop:poisson-comm} and the Pauli commutation relations $[\sigma_i,\sigma_j]=2i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k$, we compute the Poisson bracket between the coordinate functions,
\begin{equation}
	\poisson{r_i}{r_j} = \big\langle\qcomm{\sigma_i}{\sigma_j}\big\rangle = \frac{2}{\hbar}\epsilon_{ijk}r_k. \label{eq:qubit-poisson}
\end{equation}
Up to the dimensional factor $2/\hbar$, this reproduces the $\mathfrak{so}(3)$-Poisson bracket $\{r_i,r_j\}=\epsilon_{ijk}r_k$ of the classical spin system of Section~\ref{sec:1-example}. For the Riemann bracket, Proposition~\ref{prop:symmetric-bracket-intrinsic} applied to $A=\sigma_i$ and $K=\sigma_j$, combined with the Pauli identity $\sigma_i\circ\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}\Id$, yields
\begin{equation}
	((r_i,r_j)) = \frac{2}{\hbar}\big(\delta_{ij}-r_ir_j\big). \label{eq:qubit-riemann}
\end{equation}
As per Definition~\ref{def:dispersion}, the dispersion of $r_i$ is $\Delta r_i = \sqrt{\hbar/2}\sqrt{((r_i,r_i))} = \sqrt{1-r_i^2}$, which represents the spin uncertainty. Evaluating \eqref{eq:qubit-poisson} and \eqref{eq:qubit-riemann} at the north pole $\mathbf{r}=(0,0,1)$ for $f=r_1$ and $h=r_2$ yields $((f,h))=0$ and $\poisson{f}{h}=2/\hbar$. It descends that $\Delta f = \Delta h = 1$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:robertson-schrodinger} reads $1\cdot 1 \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{\hbar} = 1$. The bound is saturated at this state, illustrating the equality case discussed in the remark following Theorem~\ref{thm:robertson-schrodinger}.

Having established the geometric structure and the uncertainty relations on the Bloch sphere, we turn to the Hamiltonian dynamics generated by these observables. We focus on the evolution of a spin in a uniform magnetic field.

Consider the Hamiltonian $H=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_3$, describing a spin in a uniform magnetic field along the $z$-axis, and let $h=\langle H\rangle = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}r_3$. Using \eqref{eq:qubit-poisson} and the relation $\dot{f}=\poisson{f}{h}$ descending from the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:poisson-comm}, the equations of motion for the Bloch vector along the Hamiltonian flow of $h$ read
\begin{equation}
	\dot{r}_1 = \poisson{r_1}{h} = -\omega r_2, \qquad
	\dot{r}_2 = \poisson{r_2}{h} = \omega r_1, \qquad
	\dot{r}_3 = \poisson{r_3}{h} = 0. \label{eq:qubit-eom}
\end{equation}
The solution is a uniform precession about the $z$-axis at angular velocity $\omega$,
\begin{equation}
	r_1(t)+ir_2(t) = e^{i\omega t}\big(r_1(0)+ir_2(0)\big), \qquad r_3(t) = r_3(0), \label{eq:larmor-solution}
\end{equation}
in agreement with Proposition~\ref{prop:hamiltonian-schrodinger}. The Hamiltonian flow of $h$ on $(\mathbb{P}\mathcal{H},\omega)$ coincides with the Schrödinger evolution $[\psi]\mapsto[e^{-itH/\hbar}\psi]$, identifying the Larmor precession.

The Larmor precession exhibits a conserved quantity along the $z$-axis. To formalize the relationship between this continuous symmetry and the associated conservation law, we compute the quantum momentum map.

Let $G=SU(2)$ act on $\mathcal{H}=\C^2$ in the defining representation, with the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ spanned by the skew-adjoint generators $\xi_j:=-\frac{i}{2}\sigma_j$, such that $i\hbar\xi_j = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_j$ is the physical spin operator. By Proposition~\ref{prop:quantum-momentum-map}, the quantum momentum map for this action yields
\begin{equation}
	\langle\mu([\psi]),\xi_j\rangle = \big\langle i\hbar\xi_j\big\rangle([\psi]) = \frac{\hbar}{2}r_j([\psi]).
\end{equation}
Identifying $\mathfrak{su}(2)^*\cong\R^3$ yields $\mu = \frac{\hbar}{2}\mathbf{r}$. The momentum map corresponds to the quantum spin angular momentum vector, which acts as the $\hbar/2$-rescaled counterpart of the classical moment map $\mu_{\mathrm{cl}}(r)=-r$ of Section~\ref{sec:1-example}. For the Hamiltonian $H=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_3$ and the generator $\xi_3=-\frac{i}{2}\sigma_3$, we compute
\begin{equation}
	[H,i\hbar\xi_3] = \Big[\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_3,\frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_3\Big] = 0.
\end{equation}
Theorem~\ref{thm:quantum-noether} guarantees that the component $\mu_3=\frac{\hbar}{2}r_3$ of the momentum map is conserved along the flow, as verified in \eqref{eq:qubit-eom} where $\dot{r}_3=0$.
